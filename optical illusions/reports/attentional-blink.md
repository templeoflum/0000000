## Abstract

The attentional blink is a temporal limitation in visual attention characterized by the failure to detect a second target stimulus (T2) when it appears within approximately 200-500 milliseconds after successful detection of a first target (T1) in a rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) stream. First systematically described by Raymond, Shapiro, and Arnell in 1992, this phenomenon reveals fundamental constraints on the temporal dynamics of conscious perception and attentional resource allocation. During the attentional blink period, observers typically report being unaware of T2 even when it is clearly visible and would be easily detected outside this critical temporal window. The effect demonstrates that attention operates with significant temporal inertia, requiring time to disengage from one target and reorient to another. The magnitude of the blink varies with stimulus properties, task demands, and individual differences in attentional capacity. Notably, the deficit is specific to conscious report rather than all forms of processing, as subliminal effects of T2 can sometimes be detected even during the blink. This dissociation has made the attentional blink a valuable paradigm for investigating the relationship between attention, working memory encoding, and consciousness.
