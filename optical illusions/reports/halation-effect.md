## Abstract

The halation effect refers to the perceptual phenomenon in which bright or highly luminant regions of a visual scene appear to extend beyond their physical boundaries, causing their color and luminance to bleed or spread into adjacent darker areas. This spreading creates a halo-like appearance around bright objects, reducing perceived contrast and sharpness at luminance boundaries. The term originates from photography, where light scattering within film emulsions or digital sensors produces similar artifacts, though the perceptual halation effect occurs through distinct mechanisms in human vision. Physiologically, the effect arises from optical scatter within the ocular media, lateral inhibition patterns in retinal processing, and neural adaptation in early visual cortex. High-contrast edges between bright and dark regions are particularly susceptible, with the magnitude of spreading dependent on luminance ratios, viewing distance, and stimulus duration. The halation effect has practical implications for display design, typography, and lighting engineering, where minimizing unwanted perceptual blur is critical. Understanding this phenomenon contributes to models of spatial summation, edge detection, and the neural representation of luminance boundaries in natural scenes.
