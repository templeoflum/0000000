## Abstract

The Chubb Illusion, discovered by Charles Chubb in 1989, demonstrates that the perceived contrast of a textured region is substantially reduced when that region is embedded within a high-contrast texture surround. In the classic demonstration, a Gaussian blob or circular patch of noise texture appears to have lower contrast when surrounded by high-contrast noise compared to when surrounded by low-contrast noise or a uniform field, even though the physical contrast of the central patch remains constant. This phenomenon reveals important properties of contrast processing in the visual system, suggesting that contrast perception is normalized relative to the local context rather than computed in absolute terms. The illusion has been particularly influential in understanding second-order or texture-defined stimuli, where the relevant visual feature is defined by variations in contrast, texture, or other properties rather than by luminance variations alone. The Chubb Illusion is often explained through gain control mechanisms, whereby the sensitivity of neurons to contrast is adaptively adjusted based on the contrast statistics of the surrounding region. This normalization process allows the visual system to maintain sensitivity to relative contrast variations across a wide range of absolute contrast levels, facilitating texture segmentation and object detection under diverse viewing conditions. The illusion highlights fundamental principles of divisive normalization that appear to operate throughout the visual system.
