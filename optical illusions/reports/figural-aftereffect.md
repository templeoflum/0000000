## Abstract

The figural aftereffect, systematically characterized by Wolfgang Köhler and Hans Wallach in 1944, refers to a family of phenomena in which prolonged viewing of a figure (the inspection or adaptation figure) causes subsequently viewed test figures to appear shifted, distorted, or displaced away from the adapted position or configuration. These aftereffects can involve changes in perceived size, position, shape, orientation, or other spatial properties of test stimuli. Köhler and Wallach's original demonstrations showed that after fixating on a figure, a subsequently presented test figure appears displaced away from the location of the inspection figure, with the displacement magnitude decreasing with increasing spatial separation between inspection and test figures. They proposed that figural aftereffects arise from satiation or electrical field effects in the visual cortex, though this specific theoretical account has not been supported by subsequent research. Modern interpretations invoke neural adaptation and lateral inhibition mechanisms in populations of neurons encoding spatial position, contour location, and shape properties. Figural aftereffects have been demonstrated for numerous stimulus dimensions and provided early evidence for the adaptive and plastic nature of perceptual representations, influencing later research on tilt aftereffects, size aftereffects, and other dimension-specific adaptation phenomena.
