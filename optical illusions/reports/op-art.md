## Abstract

Op Art, short for Optical Art, refers to an artistic movement that flourished in the 1960s characterized by abstract patterns designed to create compelling visual effects such as apparent movement, vibration, flashing, warping, or afterimages in static two-dimensional images. Pioneered by artists including Bridget Riley and Victor Vasarely, Op Art systematically exploits fundamental properties of low-level visual processing, including orientation selectivity in early visual cortex, spatial frequency filtering, luminance and chromatic contrast processing, and the motion energy detection mechanisms that can be activated by certain spatial patterns even in the absence of physical movement. The artworks typically employ precise geometric patterns, high-contrast elements, repetitive structures, and careful manipulation of color relationships to maximally engage or stress specific visual processing mechanisms. Op Art demonstrates that static images can produce dynamic perceptual experiences through bottom-up activation of neural populations tuned to motion, orientation, and spatial frequency, often generating visual aftereffects, apparent depth stratification, or illusory contours. The movement represents a unique intersection of art and perceptual science, with artists empirically discovering stimulus configurations that vision scientists would later study to understand neural coding principles. Op Art continues to influence contemporary design, digital art, and remains valuable as a demonstration set for teaching principles of visual perception and the constructive nature of visual experience.
