## Abstract

The Sander's parallelogram illusion, first described by Friedrich Sander in 1926, is a geometric size illusion in which the diagonal of a larger parallelogram appears considerably longer than an objectively equal-length diagonal of a smaller parallelogram placed adjacent to or overlapping it. The illusion is typically constructed using two parallelograms of different sizes that share a common vertex or edge, with their diagonals serving as the critical comparison elements. Despite being physically identical in length, the diagonal spanning the larger parallelogram is consistently perceived as longer. Theoretical explanations invoke multiple mechanisms: the influence of surrounding context on length judgments, where the larger enclosing frame biases perception toward expansion; misapplied size constancy, where the visual system interprets the configuration as a three-dimensional scene with depth relationships; and spatial integration processes that weight local length judgments by their embedding context. The illusion demonstrates the fundamental principle that perceived size is not determined solely by retinal image dimensions but is profoundly modulated by configural context. Sander's illusion has been studied alongside related geometric illusions such as the Müller-Lyer and Ponzo illusions to understand general principles of context-dependent size perception and the role of Gestalt grouping principles in spatial judgments.
