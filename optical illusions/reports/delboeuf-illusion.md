## Abstract

The Delbœuf Illusion, discovered by Belgian physicist and psychologist Joseph Delbœuf in 1865, is a size contrast illusion in which the perceived size of a central circle is systematically influenced by the size of a surrounding concentric annulus. In the classic configuration, two identical inner circles are each surrounded by outer circles of different diameters. The inner circle surrounded by a slightly larger outer circle appears larger than the identical inner circle surrounded by a much larger outer circle. This demonstrates a distance-dependent size contrast effect: when the annulus is close in size to the target circle, assimilation occurs and the target appears larger, but when the annulus is substantially larger, contrast effects dominate and the target appears smaller. The Delbœuf Illusion reveals fundamental principles of contextual size perception and has been extensively studied to understand the spatial extent over which size information is pooled. Theoretical accounts emphasize both misapplied size constancy mechanisms, whereby concentric circles might evoke implicit depth relationships, and spatial filtering models that explain the transition from assimilation to contrast as a function of spatial separation. The illusion has practical implications, having been demonstrated to influence portion size estimation in food consumption contexts, and continues to serve as a key paradigm for understanding context-dependent size perception.
