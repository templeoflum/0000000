## Abstract

Monocular Rivalry is a perceptual phenomenon in which two or more overlapping patterns presented simultaneously to a single eye produce alternating perception, with one pattern dominating awareness while the other is suppressed, followed by spontaneous reversals. Unlike binocular rivalry, which involves competition between different images presented to different eyes, monocular rivalry demonstrates that perceptual competition and alternation can occur even when both competing stimuli fall on the same retina. This finding has important theoretical implications, indicating that rivalry is not simply a matter of interocular competition but can arise from more central mechanisms involving competition between different stimulus interpretations or feature representations. Monocular rivalry typically occurs with overlapping gratings or patterns that differ in orientation, spatial frequency, color, or motion direction. The alternations are generally slower and less complete than in binocular rivalry, with more frequent periods of mixed or piecemeal perception where both patterns are partially visible simultaneously. The phenomenon demonstrates that perceptual selection and suppression can occur at multiple levels of the visual hierarchy, not just at early stages where signals from the two eyes are first combined. Research on monocular rivalry has contributed to understanding the neural mechanisms of perceptual competition, attention, and conscious awareness, showing that rivalry reflects fundamental computational principles of competitive dynamics in cortical processing.
