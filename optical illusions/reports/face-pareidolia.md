## Abstract

Face pareidolia is the perceptual phenomenon in which observers perceive faces in non-face objects, patterns, or textures. This represents the most common and well-studied form of pareidolia, reflecting the human visual system's exceptional sensitivity to face-like configurations. Face pareidolia occurs across a wide range of stimuli, including natural objects such as rocks, clouds, and wood grain, as well as manufactured items like electrical outlets, cars, and buildings. The phenomenon is so prevalent that it occurs even with minimal cues: arrangements as simple as two dots above a curved line can trigger face perception. Face pareidolia is thought to result from specialized neural mechanisms in the fusiform face area and other face-selective cortical regions that are tuned to detect faces rapidly and with high sensitivity, even at the cost of occasional false positives. This bias toward face detection likely confers evolutionary advantages, as rapid identification of faces is crucial for social interaction and threat detection. Research using face pareidolia as a probe has revealed that illusory faces activate the same neural networks as real faces and follow similar perceptual rules, including holistic processing and attention to eye regions. The ubiquity of face pareidolia across cultures and throughout human history suggests it is a fundamental aspect of human visual cognition rather than a learned cultural phenomenon.
