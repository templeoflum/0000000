## Abstract

The Munker Illusion demonstrates a powerful assimilation effect in which target patches appear to shift in lightness or color toward the properties of thin overlying stripes, rather than showing the contrast effect predicted by simultaneous contrast principles. When identical gray or colored patches are overlaid with differently colored thin stripes, the patches appear to take on the hue or lightness of the stripes themselves. This effect is opposite to traditional simultaneous contrast, where surrounded regions shift away from their surround. Discovered by Hans Munker in 1970, the illusion reveals that the spatial scale and geometric configuration of contextual elements critically determines whether assimilation or contrast effects dominate. The Munker Illusion is particularly effective when the overlying stripes are thin relative to the target patches and when they form a regular grating pattern. The phenomenon has been explained by spatial filtering models in which low spatial frequency components drive assimilation while high spatial frequency components drive contrast. The illusion also demonstrates the importance of figure-ground segmentation and belongingness: elements that appear to belong together tend to assimilate, while segregated regions show contrast. The Munker Illusion has become a critical test case for theories of color and lightness perception, challenging simple opponent-process models and supporting multi-scale spatial filtering accounts.
