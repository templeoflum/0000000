## Abstract

The Benary Cross Illusion, first described by Wilhelm Benary in 1924, is a striking demonstration that lightness perception cannot be explained solely by local luminance ratios. The illusion consists of a black cross on a white background, with identical gray triangles positioned in two different configurations: one triangle placed on the arm of the cross (where it borders both black and white regions) and another positioned in the concave region between the cross arms (also bordering both black and white). Despite having identical luminance values and similar proportions of black and white in their immediate surrounds, the triangle on the cross arm appears substantially lighter than the triangle in the concavity. This phenomenon directly challenges simple lateral inhibition accounts of lightness perception, as these would predict identical appearances given the similar local contrast conditions. Instead, the illusion suggests that figural belongingness and spatial grouping play critical roles in lightness computation. The triangle perceived as belonging to the black cross appears lighter through a contrast effect, while the triangle perceived as belonging to the white background appears darker. The Benary Cross has been influential in developing theories of lightness perception that incorporate mid-level grouping processes and figure-ground organization, demonstrating that lightness is computed relative to perceived frameworks rather than purely local neighborhoods.
