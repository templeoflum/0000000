## Abstract

White's Illusion is a striking lightness phenomenon in which identical gray patches embedded in a square-wave grating appear different in brightness depending on whether they replace segments of the black or white bars of the grating. Crucially, the direction of the lightness difference is opposite to what classical simultaneous contrast predicts: gray patches replacing white bars appear lighter than those replacing black bars, even though the former have more black in their immediate surround and should therefore appear lighter by contrast. Discovered by Michael White in 1979, this illusion has become one of the most theoretically significant challenges to simple contrast-based accounts of lightness perception. The effect demonstrates that the visual system does not compute lightness based solely on local luminance ratios, but takes into account the extended spatial structure and orientation of edges. Gray patches are perceived as belonging to the bars they replace, and their lightness assimilates toward those bars rather than contrasting with the adjacent regions. White's Illusion has been explained by oriented spatial filtering models, edge-integration theories, and anchoring frameworks that emphasize junction geometry and contour alignment. The magnitude of the effect depends on the width of the grating bars, the size of the gray patches, and the presence of aligned versus misaligned edges, providing critical constraints on theories of lightness computation.
