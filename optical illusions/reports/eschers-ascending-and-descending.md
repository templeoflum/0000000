## Abstract

Escher's Ascending and Descending is a lithograph created by M.C. Escher in 1960 that depicts an architectural impossibility based on the Penrose stairs concept. The artwork shows a building with a continuous staircase on its rooftop where robed figures perpetually ascend or descend in an endless loop. The illusion exploits depth cue conflicts and local-to-global inconsistencies in visual processing. While each segment of the staircase appears geometrically consistent with normal Euclidean constraints, the global configuration violates fundamental principles of three-dimensional space. Viewers can follow the staircase around the quadrilateral structure and observe that it returns to its starting point while maintaining a constant incline in one direction. This creates a paradoxical closed loop where the figures would continuously climb upward or descend downward without ever changing elevation. The work demonstrates how the visual system's tendency to interpret two-dimensional contours as three-dimensional structures can be exploited when local depth cues are carefully manipulated to suggest impossible global geometries. The lithograph has become one of Escher's most recognized works and serves as a powerful demonstration of the conflict between local perceptual coherence and global geometric impossibility.
