## Abstract

The kappa effect is a temporal illusion in which the perceived duration of time intervals between sequential events is systematically influenced by the spatial separation between those events. Specifically, when two events are separated by a greater spatial distance, the temporal interval between them is perceived as longer than when the same objective time interval separates spatially closer events. This effect demonstrates a fundamental interdependence between spatial and temporal perception, revealing that the brain does not process space and time as independent dimensions but instead integrates them according to implicit assumptions about typical spatiotemporal regularities in the environment. The kappa effect has been demonstrated across various sensory modalities including vision, audition, and touch, suggesting domain-general principles of spatiotemporal integration. The phenomenon is typically explained through Bayesian frameworks in which the perceptual system assumes that greater distances require more time to traverse, leading to biased temporal judgments. The kappa effect is closely related to the tau effect, in which temporal separations influence perceived spatial distances, together forming a complementary pair of spatiotemporal illusions that reveal the bidirectional influence between space and time in perceptual processing.
