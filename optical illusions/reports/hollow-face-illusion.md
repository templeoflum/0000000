## Abstract

The Hollow-Face Illusion, also known as the Concave Face Illusion or Rotating Mask Illusion, is a robust depth inversion phenomenon in which the concave interior surface of a face mask is perceived as a normal convex face, despite available depth cues indicating the true concave structure. First systematically studied by Richard Gregory, this illusion demonstrates the extraordinary strength of top-down face-processing expectations in overriding bottom-up sensory evidence. When observers view a rotating hollow mask, it appears to be a normal face rotating in the opposite direction, with the nose appearing to protrude rather than recede. The illusion persists under binocular viewing, motion parallax, and stereoscopic presentation, making it one of the most compelling demonstrations of knowledge-driven perception. The visual system's overwhelming bias toward interpreting face-like configurations as convex reflects both evolutionary specialization for face processing and learned statistical regularities about facial structure. The effect is so powerful that it can survive even when observers are fully informed about the true concave geometry. Interestingly, individuals with schizophrenia show reduced susceptibility to the illusion, suggesting altered top-down processing in this population. The Hollow-Face Illusion has become a valuable tool for investigating the neural mechanisms of predictive coding, the interaction between sensory evidence and prior expectations, and the specialized processing afforded to faces in human vision.
