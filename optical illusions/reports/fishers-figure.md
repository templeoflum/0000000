## Abstract

Fisher's Figure is an ambiguous visual stimulus created by psychologist Gerald H. Fisher that can be perceived as two distinct three-dimensional objects depending on how the viewer organizes the visual information. The figure demonstrates principles of depth ambiguity and perceptual organization, where the same line configuration can be interpreted in multiple ways based on different assumptions about which surfaces are in front and which are behind, or how various contours connect to form coherent objects. Like other bistable figures, Fisher's Figure produces spontaneous perceptual reversals in which observers experience alternations between two stable interpretations without any change in the physical stimulus. The illusion provides insight into the constructive nature of depth perception and the role of perceptual hypotheses in resolving ambiguous visual information. Fisher's work on ambiguous figures contributed to understanding how the visual system employs organizational principles to segment images into meaningful objects and resolve inherent ambiguities in the mapping from two-dimensional retinal images to three-dimensional scene interpretation. The figure has been used in research on perceptual stability, the time course of bistable perception, and the influence of attention and prior experience on perceptual interpretation. It exemplifies the general principle that perception involves active interpretation rather than passive registration of sensory input.
