## Abstract

The coffer illusion, also known as the "16 Circles" illusion, presents observers with a pattern of rectangular shapes arranged to suggest recessed panels or coffers, as might be found in decorative architectural ceilings. Despite the predominance of rectangular forms in the image, the pattern also contains sixteen complete circles that remain hidden from most observers during casual inspection. The circles are defined by the tangent points where the rectangles meet, creating continuous curved contours that are difficult to detect because they share their orientation and spatial frequency characteristics with the rectangular framework. Once the circles are identified, they become readily visible and difficult to unsee, demonstrating a dramatic shift in figure-ground organization. The illusion illustrates how initial perceptual parsing of a scene can strongly bias subsequent interpretation, making certain organizational structures salient while rendering equally valid alternatives effectively invisible. The coffer illusion has been used to study attention, perceptual set, and the factors that govern spontaneous figure-ground segregation in complex patterns. It provides compelling evidence for the role of expectation and grouping principles in determining what features of a visual scene capture awareness.
