## Abstract

The Kinetic Depth Effect, first systematically described by Hans Wallach and D.N. O'Connell in 1953, refers to the perception of three-dimensional structure from two-dimensional motion patterns. When observers view the shadow or silhouette projection of a rotating three-dimensional object, they spontaneously perceive the object as having volumetric depth and three-dimensional form, even though the stimulus itself is a flat pattern on a two-dimensional surface. The effect demonstrates that motion information alone, without any static depth cues such as stereopsis, shading, or perspective, is sufficient to recover three-dimensional structure. The classic demonstration involves a wire frame object rotating behind a translucent screen, with only the shadow visible to the observer. As the object rotates, the changing two-dimensional projection creates systematic patterns of expansion, contraction, and relative motion that the visual system interprets as arising from a rigid three-dimensional structure moving in depth. The Kinetic Depth Effect is a foundational example of structure-from-motion perception and has been extensively studied in both psychophysics and computational vision. The phenomenon reveals sophisticated computational mechanisms that solve the ill-posed problem of recovering three-dimensional structure from ambiguous two-dimensional motion signals, typically by assuming rigidity and applying Bayesian inference to select the most likely three-dimensional interpretation.
