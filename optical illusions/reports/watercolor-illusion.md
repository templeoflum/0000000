## Abstract

The watercolor illusion, discovered by Baingio Pinna in 1987, is a chromatic completion phenomenon in which a thin colored contour flanked by a darker or differently colored contour induces an illusory tint that spreads across the entire enclosed region, resembling watercolor paint wash on paper. The effect is strongest when the lighter or more saturated color is positioned on the inner side of the contour pair, and the magnitude of spreading can extend over remarkably large spatial areas, sometimes spanning several degrees of visual angle. Unlike simultaneous contrast, which produces complementary color shifts, the watercolor illusion creates assimilative color spreading that matches the hue of the inducing contour. The illusion also influences figure-ground organization, with colored regions appearing to emerge as distinct surfaces in depth. Critical parameters include contour width (optimal around 1-2 arcmin per line), color contrast, luminance relationships, and geometric configuration. The watercolor effect demonstrates sophisticated neural mechanisms for surface interpolation and boundary assignment, revealing how the visual system constructs unified surface representations from sparse contour information. Theoretical accounts invoke filling-in processes within area V4 and higher color-processing regions, combined with mechanisms that assign ownership of chromatic signals to bounded regions. The illusion has implications for understanding artistic techniques and display technology.
