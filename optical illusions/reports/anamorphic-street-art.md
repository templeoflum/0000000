## Abstract

Anamorphic street art consists of large-scale paintings on horizontal or near-horizontal surfaces that appear dramatically three-dimensional when viewed from one specific vantage point, despite being completely flat. These artworks exploit the visual system's depth perception mechanisms by creating distorted images that, when viewed from the intended location, project the correct perspective geometry onto the retina as if viewing an actual three-dimensional scene. The technique relies on precise geometric transformations that compensate for the oblique viewing angle, effectively reversing the perspective projection to create compelling illusions of depth, including apparent holes in the ground, elevated platforms, or protruding objects. Anamorphic street art demonstrates fundamental principles of pictorial depth perception, including the dominance of linear perspective cues, the visual system's tendency to apply Bayesian inference to resolve depth ambiguity, and conflicts between monocular depth cues (perspective, relative size, height in visual field) and binocular cues (which correctly indicate a flat surface). The illusion breaks down dramatically when viewed from positions other than the intended viewpoint, revealing the extreme distortion required to achieve the effect. Contemporary street artists have popularized this technique as a form of interactive public art that creates compelling photographic opportunities.
