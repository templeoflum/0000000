## Abstract

The Café Wall Illusion is a striking geometric distortion effect in which physically parallel horizontal lines appear to converge and diverge in alternating wedge-shaped patterns. The illusion was brought to prominence by psychologist Richard Gregory in 1979 after observing it on the tiled wall of a café in Bristol, England, though earlier versions had been described by Hugo Münsterberg. The canonical stimulus consists of alternating rows of black and white rectangular tiles, with each row offset by half a tile width relative to adjacent rows, separated by thin gray mortar lines. Despite the objective parallelism of the mortar lines, observers perceive systematic tilts that create an appearance of wedge-shaped distortions. The phenomenon demonstrates the visual system's sensitivity to local orientation cues and the interactions between luminance edges and spatial filtering processes. Theoretical accounts emphasize the role of orientation-selective neurons in early visual cortex that respond to the tilted edges created at tile corners, producing biased orientation signals that propagate to influence the perceived tilt of the mortar lines. The illusion strength varies systematically with mortar width, tile aspect ratio, and luminance contrast, providing quantitative constraints on models of orientation processing and spatial integration. The Café Wall Illusion remains an important testbed for computational models of early vision.
