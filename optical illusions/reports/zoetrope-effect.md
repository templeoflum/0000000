## Abstract

The zoetrope effect refers to the perceptual phenomenon underlying the zoetrope, a cylindrical animation device in which a sequence of static images positioned around the interior of a spinning cylinder are viewed through vertical slits in the cylinder wall, producing the vivid impression of continuous motion. The device exploits fundamental properties of temporal integration in the visual system, presenting each animation frame briefly through a slit while the opaque portions of the cylinder create blank intervals that prevent motion blur and allow successive frames to be perceptually integrated into smooth apparent motion. The zoetrope, developed in the 1860s as an evolution of earlier devices like the phenakistoscope, provided compelling early evidence that motion perception does not require continuous physical movement but can be synthesized from appropriately timed sequences of discrete static images. The effect depends critically on the timing relationship between frame duration, blank interval duration, and the number of sequential images, with optimal apparent motion typically occurring when frames are presented at rates between 10-30 Hz. The zoetrope effect illustrates the same principles of persistence of vision and temporal integration that underlie modern motion picture technology, making it historically significant in both perceptual science and the development of cinema.
