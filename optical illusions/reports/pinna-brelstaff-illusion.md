## Abstract

The Pinna-Brelstaff Illusion, discovered by Baingio Pinna and G.J. Brelstaff in 2000, produces a striking perception of circular rotation in a completely static pattern when observers move toward or away from the display. The stimulus consists of concentric rings of small oriented elements, typically tilted line segments or chevrons, arranged with systematic directional biases. When the observer approaches the pattern, the rings appear to rotate in one direction; when retreating, they appear to rotate in the opposite direction. The illusion does not require eye movements or changes in vergence, occurring purely from the optic flow generated by self-motion or translational camera movement. The phenomenon demonstrates that orientation-selective mechanisms interact with motion-detection systems to extract rotational motion signals from expanding or contracting retinal image patterns. The direction of illusory rotation depends critically on the tilt angle of the individual elements relative to the radial axis, with optimal effects near 45-degree deviations. Computational models suggest that the illusion arises from the integration of local motion energy signals weighted by orientation tuning, producing a coherent percept of global rotation despite the absence of physical angular displacement. The Pinna-Brelstaff Illusion has implications for understanding optic flow processing, structure-from-motion perception, and the neural basis of self-motion estimation.
