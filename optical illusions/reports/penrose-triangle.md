## Abstract

The Penrose Triangle, also known as the Penrose Tribar or Reutersvärd Triangle, is a classic impossible object independently created by Swedish artist Oscar Reutersvärd in 1934 and later rediscovered and popularized by British psychiatrist Lionel Penrose and his son, mathematician Roger Penrose, in 1958. The figure depicts a triangular structure composed of three rectangular bars joined at right angles, creating a configuration that appears three-dimensional but cannot exist in Euclidean space. Each of the three bars appears to form a normal right-angle corner with its two adjacent bars, but the global configuration violates fundamental geometric constraints because following the structure around its complete circuit reveals contradictory depth relationships. The illusion exploits the visual system's tendency to interpret two-dimensional line drawings as three-dimensional objects using local depth cues from occlusion, junction types, and contour relationships. At each vertex, the depth ordering appears consistent with the bars meeting at ninety-degree angles. However, integrating these local interpretations around the triangle produces an impossible closed loop where each bar must simultaneously be in front of and behind adjacent bars. The Penrose Triangle gained widespread recognition after appearing in work by M.C. Escher, particularly the 1961 lithograph Waterfall, where it forms the basis for an impossible architectural structure. The figure has become one of the most extensively studied impossible objects in perceptual psychology and serves as a fundamental demonstration of how local perceptual coherence can coexist with global geometric impossibility.
