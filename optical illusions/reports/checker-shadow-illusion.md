## Abstract

The Checker Shadow Illusion, created by Edward Adelson in 1995, is one of the most powerful and counterintuitive demonstrations of lightness constancy mechanisms in human vision. The illusion depicts a checkerboard with a cylinder casting a shadow across its surface. Two squares on the checkerboard, one labeled A (outside the shadow) and one labeled B (inside the shadow), appear dramatically different in lightness: square A appears dark gray or black, while square B appears light gray or white. The striking revelation is that these two squares are physically identical in their luminance values, as can be verified by measuring their RGB values or connecting them with a uniform region. This profound perceptual discrepancy demonstrates that the visual system does not simply report retinal luminance but instead performs sophisticated computational inferences to estimate surface reflectance while discounting illumination effects. The illusion showcases the visual system's commitment to lightness constancy, the tendency to perceive surface properties as stable despite variations in illumination. Adelson's demonstration has become iconic in vision science, illustrating the constructive and inferential nature of perception and the challenges inherent in solving the inverse optics problem of recovering three-dimensional scene properties from two-dimensional retinal images.
