## Abstract

Autostereograms, popularly known as Magic Eye images, are single two-dimensional images containing repeating patterns that, when viewed with decoupled vergence and accommodation, reveal hidden three-dimensional scenes. Unlike traditional stereograms requiring side-by-side image pairs, autostereograms encode depth information within a single image through carefully controlled horizontal shifts in the repeating pattern. When observers diverge or converge their eyes to fuse non-adjacent repetitions of the pattern, the visual system interprets the varying horizontal disparities as depth variations, creating vivid perception of surfaces floating at different distances. The technique typically requires viewers to either look "through" the image (parallel viewing) or cross their eyes (cross-viewing) to achieve the necessary vergence-accommodation decoupling. The hidden three-dimensional structure emerges spontaneously once proper fusion is achieved, often accompanied by a striking phenomenological "pop-out" experience. Autostereograms became widely popular in the 1990s as commercial products and artistic demonstrations, though the ability to perceive them varies considerably across individuals. The phenomenon demonstrates several fundamental principles of stereoscopic vision, including the independence of vergence and accommodation, the visual system's tolerance for conflicting depth cues, and the computational processes underlying disparity-based depth perception. Contemporary applications include vision research, stereoacuity testing, and artistic expression.
