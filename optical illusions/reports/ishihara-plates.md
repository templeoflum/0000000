## Abstract

Ishihara plates are pseudoisochromatic color vision test stimuli consisting of colored dots arranged to form numbers or patterns that are visible to observers with normal color vision but invisible or different to observers with color vision deficiencies. Developed by Shinobu Ishihara in 1917, the test uses the principle that individuals with red-green color blindness cannot discriminate certain hue combinations that differ primarily in the red-green chromatic channel while maintaining similar luminance. Each plate contains a mosaic of dots varying in hue and luminance, with the target figure composed of dots that differ from the background primarily in chromaticity rather than brightness. To observers with normal trichromatic vision, the chromatic contrast makes the number clearly visible, but to dichromats or anomalous trichromats, the target may be invisible or may reveal a different number. The plates are designed to isolate chromatic processing from luminance processing, revealing selective deficits in specific cone photoreceptor populations or opponent color channels. Beyond clinical diagnosis, Ishihara plates demonstrate fundamental principles of color vision, including the separate processing of chromatic and achromatic information, the existence of opponent color channels, and individual differences in spectral sensitivity.
