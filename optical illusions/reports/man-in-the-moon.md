## Abstract

The Man in the Moon is a classic example of face pareidolia in which observers perceive a human face in the pattern of dark and light regions on the lunar surface visible from Earth. The dark regions, known as lunar maria (Latin for "seas"), are vast basaltic plains formed by ancient volcanic eruptions, while the lighter areas are heavily cratered highlands. When viewed from Earth, particularly during a full moon, these contrasting regions create a pattern that many cultures have interpreted as a human face, with two dark maria forming eyes and other features suggesting a nose and mouth. The perception is culturally variable: while Western traditions typically describe a face, other cultures have perceived rabbits, toads, or other figures in the same patterns. The Man in the Moon demonstrates how the human visual system imposes meaningful interpretations on randomly distributed natural features, particularly when viewing conditions involve low spatial frequency information and high contrast boundaries. The phenomenon has been documented across human history and appears in mythology, folklore, literature, and art from numerous cultures. Unlike many forms of pareidolia that depend on specific viewing angles or lighting conditions, the Man in the Moon pattern is stable across viewing sessions due to the Moon's tidally locked rotation, which always presents the same face toward Earth, making it one of the most consistently observed pareidolic images in human experience.
