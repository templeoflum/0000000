## Abstract

Mach Bands are illusory brightness enhancements and decrements perceived at the boundaries between regions of different luminance gradients. When viewing a pattern that transitions from a uniform dark region through a ramp gradient to a uniform light region, observers perceive a dark band on the dark side of the gradient and a bright band on the light side, despite these regions having uniform luminance. The phenomenon was first systematically described by physicist Ernst Mach in 1865 and has since become one of the foundational illusions in visual neuroscience. Mach Bands are classically explained by lateral inhibition mechanisms in the retina and early visual cortex, where the response of a neuron is influenced by the activity of neighboring neurons. At locations where the second spatial derivative of luminance changes sign, this lateral inhibition produces response patterns that enhance the perceived contrast. The illusion can be quantitatively modeled using difference-of-Gaussians filters or other center-surround receptive field architectures. Mach Bands demonstrate that perceived brightness is not simply a linear function of retinal luminance, but reflects spatial derivative operations performed by early visual processing. The magnitude of the bands varies with gradient steepness, spatial scale, and adaptation state, providing insights into the spatial filtering characteristics of human vision.
