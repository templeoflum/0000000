## Abstract

The McGurk effect is a striking cross-modal illusion demonstrating the integration of auditory and visual information in speech perception. Discovered by Harry McGurk and John MacDonald in 1976, the effect occurs when an auditory speech sound (for example, the syllable "ba") is dubbed onto a video of a person articulating a different sound (for example, "ga"), resulting in the perception of a third, intermediate phoneme (for example, "da") that was neither seen nor heard. This fusion demonstrates that speech perception is fundamentally multimodal, with the visual system's analysis of articulatory gestures contributing obligatorily to phonetic categorization even when auditory information is unambiguous. The effect is remarkably robust, persisting even when observers are fully aware of the audio-visual mismatch and are instructed to attend only to the auditory channel. The magnitude of the McGurk effect varies with stimulus quality, individual differences in reliance on visual speech cues, and language background. Some individuals, termed "McGurk-immune," show little or no susceptibility to the illusion, suggesting heterogeneity in multisensory integration strategies. The phenomenon has been influential in theories of multisensory perception, revealing that the brain optimally combines information across modalities, and has practical implications for understanding speech perception in noisy environments and hearing impairment.
