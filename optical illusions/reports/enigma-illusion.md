## Abstract

The Enigma Illusion, created by artist Isia Leviant in 1984, produces a compelling perception of rotary or swirling motion in a completely static image. The illusion consists of concentric rings or circular contours positioned over a background pattern of radiating lines that alternate in color or luminance. When observers view this configuration, they typically report seeing continuous circular motion within the rings, as though the pattern is rotating or flowing despite the complete absence of physical movement. The perceived motion is often described as resembling flowing water or spinning wheels. The strength and direction of the illusory motion can vary between observers and across different viewing conditions. Small eye movements and fixation patterns appear to modulate the effect, though the illusion persists even with relatively steady gaze. The Enigma Illusion demonstrates that the visual system can generate robust motion signals from purely spatial configurations, revealing fundamental principles about how orientation-selective neurons and motion-sensitive mechanisms interact. The phenomenon has generated substantial interest in vision science as a tool for investigating the neural basis of motion perception and the conditions under which static patterns can trigger dynamic perceptual experiences.
