## Abstract

Rainbows are atmospheric optical phenomena appearing as circular arcs displaying the spectral sequence of colors, caused by refraction, internal reflection, and dispersion of sunlight within spherical water droplets. The primary rainbow, appearing at approximately 42 degrees from the antisolar point, results from one internal reflection within droplets, displaying red on the outer edge and violet on the inner edge. Secondary rainbows, appearing at approximately 51 degrees with reversed color sequence, result from two internal reflections and are typically fainter than primary rainbows due to additional light loss at each reflection. The region between primary and secondary rainbows, known as Alexander's dark band, appears noticeably darker than surrounding sky due to the absence of scattered light rays in this angular range. Supernumerary rainbows are fainter colored bands appearing immediately inside the primary rainbow, caused by interference between light rays taking slightly different paths through droplets of similar size. The exact appearance of supernumerary bows depends sensitively on droplet size distribution, with monodisperse droplets producing more vivid supernumerary patterns. Rainbow phenomena have fascinated humanity throughout history, with scientific understanding progressing from Aristotle's speculations through Descartes's geometric analysis to modern wave-optical treatments incorporating diffraction and interference. The complete explanation of rainbow features requires integration of geometrical optics, physical optics, and atmospheric droplet size distributions.
