## Abstract

The size-weight illusion, first documented by Augustin Charpentier in 1891, is a robust haptic phenomenon in which smaller objects feel heavier than larger objects of identical mass when lifted. The illusion occurs despite accurate sensorimotor anticipation, as evidenced by appropriate grip force scaling based on object size prior to lift, and persists even with extensive experience and full knowledge that the objects are equally heavy. The magnitude of the illusion increases with greater size differences and is typically quantified as the point of subjective equality in psychophysical tasks, often showing that a smaller object must be 10-20 percent lighter than a larger object to be perceived as equal in weight. The illusion demonstrates a dissociation between sensorimotor control, which adapts rapidly and accurately to object properties, and conscious perception, which remains systematically biased by expectations based on the typical correlation between size and mass in natural environments. Theoretical accounts invoke Bayesian principles in which prior expectations about size-weight relationships influence the interpretation of sensory evidence. The size-weight illusion interacts with material perception, density judgments, and cognitive factors including explicit beliefs about object composition, making it a valuable paradigm for investigating the integration of visual and haptic information in object perception.
