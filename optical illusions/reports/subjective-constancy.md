## Abstract

Subjective Constancy refers to the perceptual system's fundamental tendency to maintain stable, invariant percepts of object properties despite continuous changes in proximal sensory stimulation. This general principle encompasses multiple specific constancy phenomena, including size constancy (objects appear stable in size despite changing retinal image size with distance), brightness constancy (surface reflectance appears stable despite illumination changes), color constancy (object colors appear stable across different lighting conditions), and shape constancy (three-dimensional form appears stable across viewpoint changes). Subjective constancy represents one of perception's most remarkable achievements, solving the inverse problem of inferring stable distal properties from variable proximal signals. The mechanisms underlying constancy involve integrating multiple information sources, applying learned statistical regularities about natural scenes, and implementing sophisticated computational inference. While constancy is generally adaptive, enabling stable object recognition and interaction, it can produce systematic illusions when the perceptual system's assumptions are violated or when cue conflicts arise. Theoretical accounts invoke Bayesian inference, unconscious inference processes, and predictive coding frameworks that explain how the brain combines noisy sensory data with prior expectations. Research on subjective constancy has profound implications for understanding perception as active construction rather than passive registration, revealing the inferential and interpretive nature of sensory experience.
