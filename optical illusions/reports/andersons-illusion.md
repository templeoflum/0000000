## Abstract

Anderson's Illusion is a lightness illusion that demonstrates the critical role of perceived transparency in determining surface lightness. In this illusion, two regions of identical luminance appear dramatically different in their perceived lightness when one is interpreted as being viewed through a transparent filter or overlay while the other is perceived as an opaque surface. The effect reveals how the visual system performs sophisticated computational inferences about scene geometry and material properties when determining surface reflectance. Rather than simply computing lightness from local luminance values and their immediate surrounds, the visual system appears to discount perceived transparency layers, effectively "seeing through" overlays to estimate underlying surface properties. This scission process, whereby the visual system decomposes the retinal image into separate layers of transparency and reflectance, represents a complex form of Bayesian inference about the most likely configuration of surfaces and illumination that could have produced the observed pattern. Anderson's work on this illusion has contributed significantly to understanding how the visual system solves the inverse optics problem, using assumptions about typical scene statistics and physical constraints to infer three-dimensional scene properties from two-dimensional retinal images. The illusion demonstrates that lightness perception is fundamentally inferential rather than simply determined by local contrast relationships.
