## Abstract

The Perfect Squares Illusion demonstrates systematic misperceptions of geometric regularity, in which objectively perfect squares appear distorted when embedded in specific background patterns or contextual arrangements. The squares may appear to have curved edges, non-right angles, or unequal side lengths, despite their precise mathematical construction. This phenomenon is closely related to other context-dependent geometric illusions such as the Orbison, Hering, and Zöllner illusions, all of which reveal how surrounding elements can profoundly distort the perceived shape of foreground figures. The illusion typically emerges when the square is superimposed on backgrounds containing strong orientation content, such as radiating lines, concentric circles, or tilted parallel lines, which interact with the square's edges and corners. Theoretical accounts emphasize early visual processing mechanisms, particularly the responses of orientation-selective neurons in primary visual cortex that can be biased by contextual stimuli beyond their classical receptive fields. These local orientation distortions, when integrated into a global shape percept, produce the impression of geometric deformation. The illusion highlights fundamental principles of contour integration and shape perception, demonstrating that even simple, familiar geometric forms are not perceived veridically but are constructed through processes susceptible to contextual influence. This reveals the extent to which perception depends on spatial interactions across the visual field.
