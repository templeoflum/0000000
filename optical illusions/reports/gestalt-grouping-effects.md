## Abstract

Gestalt grouping effects refer to a set of perceptual organization principles first systematically described by Gestalt psychologists in the early 20th century that determine how visual elements are grouped into coherent objects and patterns. The primary grouping principles include proximity (elements close together are grouped), similarity (elements sharing features are grouped), closure (incomplete contours are perceptually completed), continuity (elements arranged along smooth paths are grouped), common fate (elements moving together are grouped), and symmetry (symmetric elements are preferentially grouped). These principles operate automatically and pre-attentively to segment visual scenes into figures and backgrounds, enabling rapid object recognition despite ambiguous or incomplete sensory input. Gestalt grouping demonstrates that perception is not a passive process of recording sensory data but involves active organization according to built-in principles that favor simple, regular, and stable interpretations. The principles interact and compete, with their relative strengths modulated by stimulus parameters and viewing conditions. Modern computational vision research has formalized many Gestalt principles as statistical regularities that optimize perceptual inference. Understanding grouping effects is fundamental to theories of mid-level vision, texture perception, and scene segmentation.
