## Abstract

The Rabbit-Bird Illusion, also known as the "Duck-Rabbit" or "Rabbit-Duck" illusion in its most famous variant, is an ambiguous figure that can be perceived as either a rabbit or a bird depending on how visual features are organized and interpreted. In the classic version, the same contours can represent either a rabbit facing left (with long ears extending backward) or a bird facing right (with the "ears" reinterpreted as a beak). This bistable image demonstrates how identical sensory input can support radically different semantic interpretations based on alternative groupings of visual features. The perceptual switch involves a wholesale reorganization of what the depicted animal is, not merely a change in viewing angle or spatial orientation. The illusion exemplifies top-down influences in perception, as prior knowledge about animal features guides the organization of ambiguous contours into coherent object representations. Spontaneous alternations between the two interpretations reflect neural competition between incompatible perceptual hypotheses. The phenomenon has been used extensively to study semantic influences on perception, the role of expectations in object recognition, and the dynamics of perceptual bistability. Research using this illusion has illuminated how high-level conceptual knowledge interacts with low-level visual processing to construct coherent perceptual experiences from ambiguous sensory data.
