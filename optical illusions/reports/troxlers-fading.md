## Abstract

Troxler's fading, discovered by Swiss physician Ignaz Paul Vital Troxler in 1804, is a phenomenon in which peripheral visual stimuli gradually fade from conscious awareness and may disappear entirely during steady central fixation, only to reappear when eye movements occur or attention shifts. The effect is most pronounced for stimuli presented in the visual periphery while the observer maintains fixation on a central point without moving the eyes. The fading is not simply a reduction in perceived contrast but can result in complete perceptual filling-in, where the region previously occupied by the stimulus becomes perceptually indistinguishable from the background. Troxler's fading demonstrates the critical role of eye movements in maintaining stable perceptual representations; the microsaccades, drift, and tremor that normally occur during fixation continuously refresh neural responses and prevent adaptation. When these eye movements are suppressed or when a stimulus is precisely stabilized on the retina, neural adaptation in the visual pathway causes the stimulus representation to decay. The phenomenon also reveals interactions between adaptation mechanisms and attention, as attended stimuli are more resistant to fading. Troxler's fading is one of the oldest documented perceptual phenomena and has implications for understanding perceptual filling-in, neural adaptation, and the active nature of visual perception.
