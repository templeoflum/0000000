## Abstract

The gender aftereffect is a face adaptation phenomenon in which prolonged viewing of faces of one gender systematically biases the perceived gender of subsequently viewed faces in the opposite direction. After adapting to male faces, androgynous or ambiguous faces are more likely to be categorized as female, while adaptation to female faces biases perception toward male categorization. The effect demonstrates that gender perception operates via opponent coding mechanisms analogous to those in color and orientation perception, where neural populations selective for male and female facial characteristics mutually inhibit one another. The magnitude of the aftereffect depends on adaptation duration, the gender typicality of the adapting faces, and the ambiguity of test faces, with maximal effects observed for test faces near the gender boundary. The phenomenon reveals that face gender is encoded as a continuous dimension rather than a binary category, and that this encoding involves adaptive neural mechanisms that continuously calibrate to the current visual diet of faces. The gender aftereffect is specific to faces and does not transfer to non-face stimuli with gendered associations, indicating that it reflects adaptation of specialized face processing mechanisms rather than semantic or cognitive gender representations.
