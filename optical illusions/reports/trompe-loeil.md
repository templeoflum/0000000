## Abstract

Trompe-l'oeil, French for "deceive the eye," is a painting technique that creates extremely realistic imagery with such accurate perspective, lighting, and detail that viewers momentarily perceive the painted objects as three-dimensional and physically present rather than as two-dimensional representations. This artistic tradition, dating to classical antiquity and refined during the Renaissance and Baroque periods, exploits the visual system's reliance on pictorial depth cues including linear perspective, occlusion, shading, cast shadows, texture gradients, and atmospheric perspective to construct three-dimensional scene interpretations from two-dimensional retinal images. Successful trompe-l'oeil requires not only technical skill in rendering but also careful consideration of the intended viewing position, lighting conditions, and integration with the architectural or environmental context. The technique demonstrates the power of monocular depth cues to override or dominate binocular disparity information, particularly when viewing conditions limit the effectiveness of stereopsis or when expectations about the scene support the painted interpretation. Trompe-l'oeil represents perhaps the most complete exploitation of pictorial depth perception mechanisms, creating conflicts between the veridical flat surface and the compelling illusory depth that can fool viewers even when they possess explicit knowledge of the deception. The tradition continues in contemporary murals, architectural decoration, and street art, providing enduring demonstrations of the constructed nature of depth perception.
