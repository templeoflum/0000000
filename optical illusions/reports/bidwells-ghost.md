## Abstract

Bidwell's Ghost is a chromatic afterimage phenomenon in which a colored ghost image appears when a moving visual stimulus is followed by a bright achromatic flash. The classic demonstration involves a dark disk rotating against a white background, with a sector removed to allow a brief flash of light to reach the retina. When timed appropriately, observers perceive a vivid colored afterimage (the "ghost") in the region previously occupied by the moving disk, despite the disk itself being achromatic. Shelford Bidwell first described the effect in 1896 while investigating temporal aspects of visual perception. The phenomenon is related to other afterimage effects but is distinctive in requiring both motion and precise temporal synchronization between the adapting stimulus and the flash. The illusion is thought to arise from differential temporal dynamics of photoreceptor and post-receptoral adaptation processes, with the flash revealing a transient imbalance in chromatic channel responses created by the preceding stimulus. The effect demonstrates the critical importance of temporal factors in color perception and highlights how the visual system integrates information across brief time intervals. Bidwell's Ghost has been used to probe the temporal resolution of color vision and the relative time courses of different chromatic mechanisms.
