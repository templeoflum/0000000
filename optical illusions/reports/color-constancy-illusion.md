## Abstract

Color Constancy Illusions are a class of phenomena in which the visual system's mechanisms for achieving stable color perception across varying illumination conditions are systematically fooled, causing physically identical spectral compositions to appear as dramatically different colors. Under natural viewing, color constancy allows observers to perceive a banana as yellow whether viewed in bluish daylight or reddish incandescent light, by discounting the illumination and extracting surface reflectance properties. However, carefully constructed displays can exploit these same mechanisms to produce striking illusions where identical pixel values appear as entirely different hues depending on surrounding context. Famous examples include demonstrations where two patches with identical RGB values appear as blue and yellow, or brown and orange, when embedded in scenes with different inferred illumination. The illusions reveal that perceived color is not determined solely by the wavelength composition reaching the eye, but rather by sophisticated inference processes that take into account spatial context, edge information, and learned assumptions about lighting and materials. Multiple computational theories have been proposed to explain color constancy, including retinex theory, Bayesian inference frameworks, and neural network models. Color constancy illusions provide crucial tests for these theories and demonstrate both the power and limitations of the visual system's approach to solving the inverse problem of recovering surface properties from ambiguous retinal images.
