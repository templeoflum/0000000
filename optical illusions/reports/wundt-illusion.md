## Abstract

The Wundt illusion, first described by pioneering psychologist Wilhelm Wundt in 1898, is a geometric distortion illusion in which two parallel horizontal lines appear to bow inward (become concave) when superimposed on a background of converging radial lines. The illusion is the inverse of the Hering illusion, in which parallel lines appear to bow outward against a background of diverging lines. In the Wundt configuration, the radial lines typically converge toward a central point beyond the parallel test lines, creating a visual context that systematically distorts perceived straightness. The magnitude of curvature illusion depends on the density, length, and angle of the inducing radial lines, as well as the spatial separation between the test lines. Theoretical accounts invoke mechanisms of orientation contrast and spatial filtering: local orientations in the radial background interact with edge detectors processing the test lines, biasing their perceived trajectory. Neural models based on inhibitory interactions between orientation-selective neurons in early visual cortex can reproduce the illusion's essential features. The Wundt illusion, along with its inverse the Hering illusion and related phenomena such as the Zöllner and Poggendorff illusions, has been instrumental in developing theories of contour processing, orientation coding, and contextual modulation in visual perception. It demonstrates that perceived straightness is not simply encoded locally but emerges from interactions across spatial scales.
