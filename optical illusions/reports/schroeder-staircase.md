## Abstract

The Schroeder Staircase, first described by German scientist Heinrich G.F. Schroeder in 1858, is an ambiguous figure depicting a staircase that spontaneously reverses its apparent spatial orientation during viewing. The drawing can be perceived either as stairs viewed from above, ascending from lower left to upper right, or as stairs viewed from below, descending into the picture plane. This bistable depth reversal occurs because the two-dimensional line drawing lacks definitive depth cues that would specify the correct three-dimensional interpretation, allowing the visual system to construct two equally plausible spatial configurations from the same contour information. When the percept switches, the entire three-dimensional structure reorganizes, with surfaces that appeared as stair treads becoming risers and vice versa. The phenomenon demonstrates fundamental principles of depth perception and the active nature of three-dimensional scene interpretation from two-dimensional images. The Schroeder Staircase represents one of the earliest documented examples of bistable depth perception and has been important for studying the mechanisms underlying ambiguous figure perception. Research using this and related depth-reversible stimuli has illuminated how the visual system selects among competing spatial interpretations, the role of attention in stabilizing percepts, and the neural dynamics of perceptual alternations in the representation of three-dimensional space.
