## Abstract

Fechner colors are subjective chromatic percepts that arise when viewing rotating black-and-white patterns at specific angular velocities, typically between 5 and 10 revolutions per second. Named after Gustav Fechner, who first systematically described the phenomenon in 1838, these illusory colors appear despite the complete absence of chromatic information in the stimulus. The colors perceived vary with rotation speed, pattern configuration, and individual observer characteristics, often manifesting as concentric rings or radial bands of pastel hues including reds, greens, blues, and yellows. The illusion demonstrates that color perception can emerge from purely temporal and spatial luminance variations rather than requiring differential activation of cone photoreceptors by different wavelengths. Several theoretical accounts have been proposed, including differential latencies in neural pathways processing light and dark stimuli, temporal interactions between magnocellular and parvocellular visual streams, and phase differences in photoreceptor responses to flickering stimuli. The phenomenon remains incompletely understood and continues to inform contemporary models of temporal processing in early vision. Fechner colors represent a striking dissociation between physical stimulus properties and perceptual experience, revealing the constructive nature of chromatic sensation.
