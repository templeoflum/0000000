## Abstract

Aliasing refers to a class of visual artifacts that arise when continuous spatial or temporal signals are undersampled, violating the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem. In visual contexts, aliasing manifests as moiré patterns, staircase edges (jaggies), wagon-wheel effects, and other spurious structures that were not present in the original scene. These artifacts occur when the spatial frequency content of an image exceeds half the sampling rate of the display or imaging system, causing high-frequency components to be misrepresented as lower frequencies. Common examples include the shimmering patterns seen when photographing fine textures like brick walls or fabric weaves, the jerky rotation reversal of wheels in films, and the pixelated edges of diagonal lines on low-resolution displays. While aliasing is fundamentally a limitation of discrete sampling systems rather than a perceptual phenomenon, the visual system's spatial filtering properties interact with these artifacts in complex ways. Anti-aliasing techniques in computer graphics and image processing attempt to mitigate these effects through various filtering and supersampling strategies. Understanding aliasing is essential for digital imaging, display technology, and computer vision applications.
