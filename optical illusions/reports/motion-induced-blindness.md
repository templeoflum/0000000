## Abstract

Motion-Induced Blindness (MIB) is a striking perceptual phenomenon in which salient, stationary visual targets spontaneously and repeatedly disappear from conscious awareness when superimposed on a moving background pattern. Discovered by Yoram Bonneh and colleagues in 2001, MIB demonstrates that physical salience does not guarantee perceptual persistence, and that motion processing can actively suppress static feature detection. The illusion typically manifests when observers fixate centrally while attending to high-contrast static targets overlaid on a rotating or translating pattern of distractors. Individual targets disappear unpredictably for several seconds before spontaneously reappearing, creating a dynamic pattern of perceptual fading and recovery. Unlike other forms of perceptual suppression such as binocular rivalry or Troxler fading, MIB requires no interocular conflict and occurs despite maintained fixation and attention. The phenomenon has been attributed to interactions between motion-processing mechanisms and surface segmentation processes, with theoretical accounts emphasizing attentional capture by global motion signals, neural adaptation, or competitive dynamics in hierarchical visual representations. MIB provides a powerful tool for investigating the neural correlates of consciousness and the relationship between attention and awareness.
