## Abstract

The McCollough effect, discovered by Celeste McCollough in 1965, is a remarkable orientation-contingent color aftereffect in which adaptation to colored gratings of different orientations causes achromatic gratings to appear tinted in complementary colors depending on their orientation. The classic demonstration involves alternately viewing vertical red-and-black gratings and horizontal green-and-black gratings for several minutes. Subsequently, vertical black-and-white gratings appear greenish-cyan, while horizontal black-and-white gratings appear pinkish-red. What makes this aftereffect extraordinary is its persistence, lasting hours, days, or even weeks after a brief adaptation period, far exceeding the duration of conventional afterimages or simple adaptation effects. The phenomenon demonstrates that the visual system can adapt to conjunctions of features (color and orientation) at sites where these dimensions are jointly encoded, likely in populations of neurons in early visual cortex (V1) that are simultaneously selective for both color and orientation. The McCollough effect has been extensively studied and has generated numerous theoretical debates regarding its neural basis, the role of associative learning versus pure adaptation, and why it exhibits such unusual temporal properties. It remains one of the most intensively investigated and theoretically significant phenomena in vision science.
