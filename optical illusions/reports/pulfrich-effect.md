## Abstract

The Pulfrich Effect is a stereoscopic depth illusion discovered by German physicist Carl Pulfrich in 1922, in which an object moving laterally across the visual field appears to follow an elliptical path in depth when one eye receives reduced luminance input, typically through a neutral density filter. The illusion arises from the differential processing latency between the two eyes: dimmer images require longer neural processing time than brighter images. When a moving object is viewed with asymmetric luminance between the eyes, the temporal delay in the dimmed eye's signal causes the visual system to register the object's position at slightly different spatial locations for each eye, creating a spurious binocular disparity signal. The brain interprets this artificial disparity as depth information, causing the object to appear closer when moving in one direction and farther when moving in the opposite direction. The magnitude of the perceived depth excursion is proportional to both the luminance reduction and the velocity of the moving object. The Pulfrich Effect reveals fundamental principles about the temporal dynamics of visual processing and demonstrates how the brain uses timing differences between the eyes to construct depth perception. This phenomenon has clinical relevance for patients with asymmetric optic nerve damage and has been exploited in certain three-dimensional television and cinema presentations.
