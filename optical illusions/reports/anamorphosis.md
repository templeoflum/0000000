## Abstract

Anamorphosis refers to a class of perspective techniques that produce images appearing severely distorted when viewed frontally but resolving into recognizable scenes when observed from a specific oblique angle or reflected in a curved mirror. Dating back to the Renaissance period, anamorphic art exploits the geometric principles of linear perspective in reverse, deliberately creating extreme foreshortening or curvilinear distortion that becomes perceptually corrected only from the intended viewpoint. The technique exists in two primary forms: perspective anamorphosis, requiring viewing from an acute angle to the picture plane, and mirror anamorphosis, requiring reflection in a cylindrical or conical mirror positioned at a specific location. When viewed from the correct vantage point, the brain's perspective correction mechanisms interpret the distorted image as a normal three-dimensional scene, demonstrating the visual system's remarkable ability to achieve shape and size constancy across varying viewing geometries. Anamorphic art has been employed for artistic purposes, religious symbolism, political commentary, and entertainment throughout history. Modern applications include street art that appears three-dimensional from designated viewing positions and distortion correction in wide-angle projection systems. The phenomenon illustrates fundamental principles of projective geometry and the computational strategies the visual system employs to infer three-dimensional structure from two-dimensional retinal images.
