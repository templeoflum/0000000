## Abstract

The Simultaneous Contrast Illusion is the foundational phenomenon in lightness perception whereby an identical gray patch appears lighter when surrounded by a dark background and darker when surrounded by a light background. First systematically described by French chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul in 1839, this illusion demonstrates that perceived lightness is not determined solely by the absolute luminance of a surface, but by its relationship to surrounding luminance values. The effect is "simultaneous" because the contrast occurs when both the target and surround are viewed at the same time, distinguishing it from successive contrast effects that occur after temporal adaptation. Simultaneous contrast has been explained by multiple mechanisms operating at different levels of visual processing. At the retinal and early cortical levels, lateral inhibition among neurons with center-surround receptive fields amplifies luminance differences at edges and boundaries. At higher levels, lightness computation mechanisms interpret luminance patterns in terms of surface reflectance and illumination, with surrounds providing contextual information about likely lighting conditions. The magnitude of simultaneous contrast varies with factors including the spatial extent of targets and surrounds, edge sharpness, chromatic properties, and three-dimensional scene interpretation. This illusion has been central to virtually every major theory of lightness and brightness perception and remains a crucial phenomenon for understanding how the visual system achieves approximate constancy of surface appearance across varying illumination.
