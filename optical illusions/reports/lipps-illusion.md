## Abstract

The Lipps Illusion is a geometric illusion described by German philosopher and psychologist Theodor Lipps, involving systematic misperceptions of line lengths, angles, or spatial relationships in two-dimensional figures. Lipps, who worked extensively on psychological aesthetics and spatial perception in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, contributed to the catalog of classical geometric illusions that revealed fundamental properties of human spatial vision. While the precise year of discovery and specific stimulus configuration are not well-established in contemporary literature, the illusion bears Lipps's name in recognition of his contributions to early experimental psychology and perception research. Like other geometric illusions from this era, the Lipps Illusion likely involves contextual effects where surrounding elements bias the perceived properties of target lines or shapes. Such effects typically arise from orientation-selective processing and spatial filtering mechanisms in early visual cortex, where lateral interactions among neurons representing different spatial locations and orientations can produce systematic distortions. The illusion contributes to the historical foundation of perceptual science by demonstrating that perception is constructive rather than veridical, and that even simple geometric properties are subject to contextual modulation. Further archival research is needed to establish the precise parameters and historical context of this illusion.
