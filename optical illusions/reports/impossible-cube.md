## Abstract

The Impossible Cube is an impossible object depicting a cube-like structure where edges connect in ways that violate the constraints of three-dimensional Euclidean geometry. The figure typically shows a wire-frame or solid cube where certain edges appear to connect front faces to back faces in contradictory ways, creating depth relationships that cannot be consistently maintained throughout the structure. Unlike the bistable Necker cube, which allows two valid three-dimensional interpretations, the Impossible Cube presents a configuration where local depth cues suggest relationships that are globally inconsistent. Viewers perceive each face and edge as geometrically plausible in isolation, but attempting to trace the connections around the entire structure reveals violations of spatial coherence. The illusion exploits the visual system's use of junction types, occlusion cues, and line configurations to infer three-dimensional structure from two-dimensional projections. By carefully constructing junctions that provide conflicting depth information, the figure maintains local perceptual coherence while producing global impossibility. The Impossible Cube has appeared in various artistic contexts, including M.C. Escher's lithograph Belvedere, where a figure holds such an object. The figure serves as a fundamental example of how two-dimensional representations can suggest three-dimensional interpretations that are internally contradictory, revealing the constructive nature of depth perception and the limitations of local-to-global integration in visual processing.
