## Abstract

The Reversing Rotation Illusion refers to a class of ambiguous motion displays where a continuously rotating object spontaneously appears to reverse its direction of rotation despite unchanging physical motion. This phenomenon typically occurs with depth-ambiguous stimuli such as rotating cylinders, spheres with surface markings, or kinetic depth displays where the three-dimensional structure must be inferred from two-dimensional motion patterns. The illusion arises because the projected two-dimensional motion on the retina is compatible with multiple three-dimensional rotation directions—for example, a cylinder rotating clockwise when viewed from one side appears identical to the same cylinder rotating counterclockwise when viewed from the opposite side. Without definitive depth cues specifying the viewing angle, the visual system alternates between these equally valid interpretations, resulting in spontaneous perceptual reversals every few seconds. The phenomenon demonstrates how motion perception involves active inference about three-dimensional structure from ambiguous two-dimensional input. Factors including attention, eye movements, and prior viewing history influence the timing and direction of reversals. Research on reversing rotation has provided insights into the neural mechanisms of motion perception, depth-from-motion processing, and the dynamics of perceptual bistability in the motion domain. The illusion remains important for understanding how the visual system resolves ambiguity in structure-from-motion perception.
