## Abstract

The Fraser Spiral Illusion, also known as the Twisted Cord Illusion or False Spiral, is a striking geometric illusion in which a series of concentric circles appears to form an outward-spiraling pattern. Discovered by British psychologist James Fraser in 1908, the illusion is created by overlaying tilted or rotated elements—typically small line segments, arcs, or checkered patterns—onto circular arcs. Despite the underlying structure being perfectly circular, observers reliably perceive a continuous spiral that seems to wind outward from the center. The effect is so compelling that even after tracing the circles with a finger or pointer, the spiral percept persists. The illusion arises from local orientation cues that bias contour integration processes, causing the visual system to misinterpret the global shape. Orientation-selective neurons in early visual cortex respond preferentially to the tilted elements, and their activity influences how the brain connects local edge segments into continuous contours. The Fraser Spiral demonstrates the constructive nature of perception and the dominance of local feature processing over veridical shape representation. It has been widely used in perceptual research to study contour integration, grouping, and the interaction between local and global visual processing.
