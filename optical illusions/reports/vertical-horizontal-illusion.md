## Abstract

The vertical-horizontal illusion is one of the oldest and most extensively studied geometric illusions, in which a vertical line appears longer than a horizontal line of equal physical length. The illusion manifests across diverse configurations, including isolated line pairs, L-shapes, T-shapes, and inverted-T arrangements, with typical overestimation of vertical extent ranging from 10 to 30 percent depending on stimulus parameters and viewing conditions. The effect is robust across cultures, age groups, and species, suggesting deep roots in visual system architecture. Multiple theoretical accounts have been proposed: anisotropy in early cortical representation, where vertical and horizontal orientations are processed differently by orientation-selective neurons in V1; oculomotor or attentional scanning biases that favor vertical exploration; and misapplied size constancy, where vertical extents are interpreted as receding in depth and thus scaled upward. Empirical evidence supports contributions from multiple mechanisms, with the relative weight of each depending on stimulus context and task demands. The illusion has been linked to related phenomena including the Fick illusion, T illusion, and L illusion, all of which involve orientation-dependent length distortions. Understanding the vertical-horizontal illusion has implications for theories of spatial vision, anisotropic neural coding, and the interaction between retinal and cortical factors in shaping perception of metric properties.
