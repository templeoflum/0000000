## Abstract

Hidden Face Illusions are a class of visual stimuli in which human faces are concealed or camouflaged within complex scenes, becoming apparent only after the observer discovers the appropriate perceptual organization. Unlike bistable figures where multiple interpretations alternate, hidden face illusions typically produce a one-way perceptual transition: once the face is discovered, it becomes difficult to unsee it, demonstrating the powerful influence of face-detection mechanisms on visual perception. These illusions exploit the human visual system's heightened sensitivity to face-like configurations, which is mediated by specialized neural circuitry including the fusiform face area. The faces may be embedded in landscapes, architecture, arrangements of objects, or abstract patterns, requiring specific figure-ground assignments or gestalt groupings to become visible. Historical examples include Giuseppe Arcimboldo's composite portraits and various puzzle images popular in the 19th and 20th centuries. The effectiveness of these illusions relates to the brain's face-processing bias, where even minimal configurations suggesting eyes, nose, and mouth can trigger face perception. Once attention is drawn to the critical features and the appropriate perceptual organization is achieved, top-down processing maintains the face percept. Hidden face illusions demonstrate both the automaticity and the constructive nature of face perception, showing how specialized recognition systems can extract meaningful patterns from ambiguous or degraded visual information.
