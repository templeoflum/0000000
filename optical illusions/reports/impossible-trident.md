## Abstract

The Impossible Trident, also known as the Blivet or Devil's Tuning Fork, is a classic impossible object first described by D.H. Schuster in 1964. The figure depicts a three-pronged fork-like structure where three cylindrical prongs at one end paradoxically merge into two rectangular bars at the opposite end without any visible branching or joining. The illusion exploits fundamental ambiguities in figure-ground segregation and depth interpretation from two-dimensional line drawings. At the pronged end, three distinct circular cross-sections clearly indicate three separate cylindrical tines. However, following these prongs toward the base reveals only two flat rectangular supports separated by a single gap. The middle region of the figure contains the critical ambiguity where the same contours can be interpreted either as edges of solid cylinders or as boundaries of empty spaces between surfaces. The visual system cannot maintain a globally consistent interpretation because assigning figure-ground relationships that work for one end of the object necessarily create contradictions at the other end. This impossible transformation demonstrates how local depth cues can appear individually plausible while producing global inconsistencies. The Impossible Trident became widely known after appearing in various publications in the 1960s and has since become one of the most recognizable impossible figures. The design has been extensively used in perceptual psychology research examining depth perception, object recognition, and the integration of local visual features into coherent global interpretations.
