## Abstract

The hue shift illusion encompasses a class of chromatic induction phenomena in which the perceived hue of a target region shifts systematically as a function of the chromaticity of surrounding or adjacent regions. This effect demonstrates that color perception is fundamentally contextual, with the visual system computing local color appearance through interactions between neighboring chromatic signals rather than processing wavelength information in isolation. The magnitude and direction of hue shifts depend on multiple factors including the chromatic distance between target and inducing regions, spatial configuration, temporal dynamics, and luminance relationships. Classic examples include simultaneous color contrast, where a gray patch appears tinged with the complementary color of its surround, and chromatic assimilation effects under specific spatial conditions. Neural mechanisms underlying hue shifts involve lateral interactions in early visual cortex, particularly in color-opponent channels, as well as higher-level normalization processes that extract surface reflectance properties independent of illumination. The hue shift illusion has been central to developing computational models of color constancy and understanding how the visual system achieves stable color perception across varying viewing conditions. Quantitative measurements reveal shifts of 10-30 degrees in hue angle under strong induction conditions.
