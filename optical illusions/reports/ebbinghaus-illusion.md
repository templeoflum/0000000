## Abstract

The Ebbinghaus Illusion, also known as Titchener Circles, is one of the most studied size contrast illusions in perceptual psychology. Discovered by German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus in 1902, the illusion presents two identical central circles, one surrounded by an array of large circles and the other by small circles. The central circle surrounded by large circles appears substantially smaller than the identical central circle surrounded by small circles, demonstrating powerful contextual modulation of perceived size. The magnitude of the effect varies systematically with the size ratio between central and surrounding circles, their spatial proximity, and the number of surrounding elements. This illusion has become a paradigmatic example of context-dependent perception and has been central to debates about the modularity of vision, particularly regarding whether perceptual illusions persist in visually guided action tasks. Theoretical accounts emphasize size contrast mechanisms whereby surrounding elements serve as a reference frame that biases size estimates, as well as misapplied size constancy interpretations in which surrounding circles implicitly suggest depth relationships. Spatial filtering models provide quantitative predictions based on center-surround receptive field properties in early visual cortex. The Ebbinghaus Illusion has been used extensively to investigate perception-action dissociations, individual differences in visual processing, and cross-cultural variations in susceptibility to geometric illusions.
