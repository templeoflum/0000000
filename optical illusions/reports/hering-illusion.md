## Abstract

The Hering Illusion is a classical geometric distortion illusion in which two parallel straight lines appear to bow outward when superimposed on a background of radiating lines emanating from a central point. Discovered by German physiologist Ewald Hering in 1861, this illusion demonstrates how contextual orientation information can systematically distort the perception of straightness and parallelism. The radiating background lines create a strong impression of depth or perspective, resembling the visual pattern produced when looking down a corridor or tunnel. The parallel test lines, when placed across this radial pattern, appear curved or bowed outward, with maximum distortion typically occurring near the center of the radial pattern. The illusion is traditionally explained by orientation contrast effects in early visual cortex, where orientation-selective neurons responding to the test lines are influenced by lateral interactions with neurons responding to the nearby radial elements. Alternative accounts invoke misapplied size constancy, where the visual system interprets the radial pattern as a depth cue and incorrectly applies perspective corrections to the test lines. The Hering Illusion is complementary to the Wundt Illusion, where the same parallel lines appear to bow inward when placed on a different contextual pattern, demonstrating the powerful role of contextual orientation in shape perception.
