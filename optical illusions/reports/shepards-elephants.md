## Abstract

Shepard's Elephants is an impossible figure created by cognitive scientist Roger Shepard depicting elephants where the legs and the spaces between legs are perceptually ambiguous and mutually incompatible. The illustration typically shows a row of elephants where careful examination reveals that it is impossible to determine which gray regions represent solid elephant legs versus which regions represent empty spaces between the legs. The figure exploits figure-ground segregation processes, where the visual system must determine which areas of an image correspond to objects versus background. In normal perception, clear depth and occlusion cues disambiguate figure from ground, but Shepard's design carefully constructs regions where the same contours can serve as boundaries for either interpretation. Following the elephants from head to tail, viewers perceive coherent elephant bodies with recognizable features. However, attempting to trace individual legs to the ground reveals contradictions where legs appear to merge, split, or transform into the spaces between other legs in impossible ways. The illusion demonstrates how local perceptual coherence can be maintained while global consistency is violated. Each elephant appears complete and well-formed when attention focuses on its head and body, but examining the leg region reveals the impossibility. This figure belongs to a broader class of Shepard's impossible figures that exploit ambiguities in visual interpretation, including his famous turning tables illusion. The work exemplifies how artistic manipulation of figure-ground relationships can create compelling visual paradoxes that reveal fundamental properties of perceptual organization.
