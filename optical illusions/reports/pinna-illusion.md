## Abstract

The Pinna Illusion generates a compelling perception of rotation in static concentric circular patterns composed of tilted micropatterns. Discovered by Italian vision scientist Baingio Pinna, the illusion occurs when small oriented elements such as line segments, squares, or chevrons are arranged along concentric rings with systematic tilt variations. Although the pattern contains no physical motion, observers perceive spontaneous rotation or counter-rotation of the rings, with inner and outer rings often appearing to spin in opposite directions. The effect is typically most vivid when viewing the pattern in peripheral vision or during brief fixational eye movements. The direction and strength of the illusory rotation depend critically on the orientation gradient of the constituent elements and their spatial arrangement. Unlike motion aftereffects that require prior adaptation, the Pinna Illusion emerges immediately upon viewing and persists under sustained observation. Theoretical explanations emphasize the role of orientation-selective filters in early visual cortex that interact with motion-detection mechanisms, potentially through cross-orientation suppression or through the analysis of oriented contrast boundaries that simulate motion trajectories. The illusion has become a valuable tool for probing the relationship between static form analysis and dynamic motion perception, revealing how orientation processing contributes to motion signals even in the absence of physical displacement.
