## Abstract

Chromostereopsis is a depth illusion in which objects of different colors, presented at the same physical distance and viewed binocularly, appear to be located at different depths. The effect is most pronounced for red and blue stimuli, with red typically appearing closer than blue for most observers, though a minority experience the opposite. The phenomenon arises primarily from longitudinal chromatic aberration in the eye's optics, which causes different wavelengths to focus at slightly different distances behind the cornea. Blue light, having a shorter wavelength, is refracted more strongly and focuses in front of the retina, while red light focuses behind it. This differential defocus creates slightly different retinal image sizes and positions for the two eyes, which the visual system interprets as a binocular disparity signal indicating depth separation. The magnitude of the effect varies substantially across individuals depending on pupil size, accommodation state, and individual differences in ocular chromatics. Chromostereopsis can also be influenced by cognitive factors and learned associations between color and depth. The illusion has practical implications for display design, data visualization, and user interface development, where chromatic choices can inadvertently create misleading depth impressions. It also demonstrates how optical imperfections in the eye contribute to perceptual phenomena alongside neural processing mechanisms.
