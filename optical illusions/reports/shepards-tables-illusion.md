## Abstract

Shepard's Tables illusion, created by cognitive scientist Roger N. Shepard in 1990, is a dramatic demonstration of how perspective cues override metric perception of shape. The stimulus consists of two identical parallelograms depicted as tabletops viewed from different angles in three-dimensional space. Despite being objectively congruent in their two-dimensional projection, one table appears long and narrow while the other appears short and wide, creating a compelling impression of radically different proportions. The illusion arises because the visual system automatically interprets the parallelograms as rectangular surfaces slanted in depth according to the rules of linear perspective. Size constancy mechanisms then scale the perceived dimensions to compensate for the presumed foreshortening, resulting in dramatic shape distortions. Even when observers are explicitly told the shapes are identical, or when the tabletops are overlaid to demonstrate their congruence, the perceptual difference persists, highlighting the impermeability of early visual processing to high-level knowledge. Shepard's Tables has become one of the most iconic modern illusions, widely reproduced in textbooks and popular media. It exemplifies the fundamental principle that perception is an active interpretation of sensory data rather than a passive registration, and demonstrates the powerful influence of implicit three-dimensional scene assumptions on two-dimensional shape judgments.
