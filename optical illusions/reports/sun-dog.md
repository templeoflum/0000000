## Abstract

Sun dogs, also known as parhelia or mock suns, are atmospheric optical phenomena appearing as bright spots or patches of light flanking the sun at approximately 22 degrees to either side, caused by refraction of sunlight through hexagonal plate-shaped ice crystals with their principal axes oriented vertically. These crystals act as 60-degree prisms, refracting light that passes through their vertical side faces with a minimum deviation angle of approximately 22 degrees. Sun dogs are most commonly observed when the sun is relatively low in the sky, as this geometry maximizes the number of appropriately oriented ice crystals contributing to the phenomenon. They often display spectral coloration, with red on the side nearest the sun and blue or white on the outer edge, though atmospheric scattering and overlap can reduce color saturation. Sun dogs typically appear brighter than 22-degree halos and may extend into horizontal tails pointing away from the sun when crystals are distributed across a range of orientations. The phenomenon has been documented throughout human history, often associated with weather predictions since the cirrus clouds required for sun dog formation frequently precede frontal systems. The term "sun dog" may derive from the appearance of these bright spots following the sun across the sky like faithful companions, while the technical term "parhelion" comes from Greek meaning "beside the sun." Understanding sun dogs requires knowledge of ice crystal optics, atmospheric crystal orientation distributions, and geometric ray tracing.
