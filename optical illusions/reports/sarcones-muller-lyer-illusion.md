## Abstract

Sarcone's Müller-Lyer illusion is a contemporary variant of the classic Müller-Lyer illusion created by visual artist and cognitive researcher Gianni Sarcone. Rather than using traditional line segments with inward- or outward-pointing arrowheads, Sarcone's version employs filled shapes, solid geometric forms, or area-based elements to produce comparable length distortions. The innovation lies in demonstrating that the Müller-Lyer effect does not depend critically on the presence of explicit linear fins or angles, but emerges from more general spatial relationships involving boundary configuration and figural extent. In typical implementations, shapes such as rectangles, triangles, or composite polygons are arranged such that identical linear extents embedded within different surrounding contexts appear unequal in length. This reformulation challenges purely angle-based explanations of the classic Müller-Lyer illusion and supports theories emphasizing spatial filtering, centroid computation, or holistic configural processing. Sarcone's variant has pedagogical value in illustrating the robustness of context effects on size perception across diverse stimulus formats, and contributes to ongoing theoretical debates about whether geometric illusions arise from low-level visual mechanisms, higher-level cognitive interpretations, or an interaction of both. The illusion demonstrates the creative potential of modern illusion design to probe and extend classic perceptual phenomena.
