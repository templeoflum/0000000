## Abstract

The Ambiguous Columns Illusion is a bistable perceptual phenomenon in which a visual depiction of columns or cylindrical forms spontaneously alternates between appearing convex (protruding outward) and concave (recessed inward). This ambiguity arises from conflicting depth cues in the stimulus, particularly shading gradients that can be interpreted as either illumination from above revealing convex surfaces or illumination revealing concave surfaces. The visual system typically assumes light comes from above, but when this assumption is challenged or when the stimulus provides insufficient disambiguating information, perception alternates between the two competing interpretations. The illusion demonstrates the brain's active role in constructing three-dimensional percepts from two-dimensional retinal images and highlights the importance of prior assumptions about lighting direction in depth perception. Observers report involuntary perceptual switches between the convex and concave interpretations, with individual switch rates varying considerably. The phenomenon is related to other depth-ambiguous figures and provides insight into the neural mechanisms underlying bistable perception, including the role of competitive dynamics in visual processing and the influence of top-down expectations on perceptual organization.
