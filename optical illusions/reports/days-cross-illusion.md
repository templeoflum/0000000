## Abstract

Day's Cross Illusion is a geometric length distortion effect in which the perceived length of the arms of a cross figure varies systematically with their relative proportions, despite objective equality. Identified by Australian psychologist Ross Day, the illusion demonstrates that identical line segments forming the horizontal and vertical arms of a cross appear unequal when the overall proportions of the cross are manipulated. Specifically, when one arm of the cross is extended or shortened relative to the perpendicular arm, the apparent length of both arms shifts in a predictable manner. This phenomenon reveals how the visual system's length judgments are influenced by global configuration and relative proportions rather than being based solely on local metric information. Theoretical accounts of Day's Cross Illusion emphasize the role of misapplied size constancy mechanisms, whereby the visual system treats certain configurations as implicit depth or perspective cues, leading to inappropriate scaling of perceived length. Alternative explanations focus on orientation-dependent pooling of spatial information and asymmetries in how the visual system represents horizontal versus vertical extents. The illusion has been studied through systematic variations in arm length ratios, overall cross size, and orientation, revealing robust effects that persist across a range of stimulus parameters and providing insights into the integration of local and global information in length perception.
