## Abstract

The anorthoscope is a device invented by Joseph Plateau in 1836 that demonstrates the visual system's capacity for temporal integration across sequential glimpses of visual information. The apparatus consists of a distorted or fragmented image mounted on a rotating disc, viewed through a narrow vertical slit. As the disc rotates behind the stationary slit, different portions of the distorted image are revealed in rapid succession. Despite seeing only thin vertical slices at any given moment, observers perceive a coherent, undistorted image. This phenomenon reveals that the visual system can integrate spatially discontinuous information over time, storing brief glimpses and synthesizing them into a unified percept. The anorthoscope provided early experimental evidence for retinal persistence and temporal integration mechanisms, challenging the notion that perception operates purely on instantaneous sensory input. The device shares functional principles with other early animation technologies like the phenakistoscope and zoetrope, all of which exploit the visual system's temporal integration window to create perceptual continuity from discrete visual samples. The anorthoscope remains significant in the history of vision science as one of the first demonstrations that perception involves active temporal binding of sequential information.
