## Abstract

The Knill and Kersten Illusion demonstrates how the visual system integrates three-dimensional shape information with lighting interpretation to determine surface lightness. In this illusion, two regions of identical luminance appear dramatically different in perceived lightness based on their position within a three-dimensionally interpreted scene. The effect arises when the visual system infers different lighting conditions or surface orientations for the two regions, leading to different reflectance estimates despite identical retinal stimulation. This illusion provides compelling evidence for Bayesian approaches to vision, where the perceptual system combines uncertain sensory data with prior assumptions about physical scenes to arrive at the most probable interpretation. The phenomenon highlights that lightness perception is not simply a function of local luminance ratios, but involves sophisticated inference about distal scene properties including surface geometry, illumination direction, and material properties. The Knill and Kersten Illusion has been particularly influential in computational vision research, demonstrating that human perception approximates optimal statistical inference when disambiguating sensory input. The strength of the effect varies with the clarity of depth cues and the plausibility of the implied lighting configuration.
