## Abstract

Escher's Relativity is a 1953 lithograph by M.C. Escher that depicts an architectural space with three distinct gravitational orientations coexisting impossibly within a single structure. The artwork shows multiple human figures inhabiting a building with interconnected staircases and doorways, where each figure appears to experience gravity in a different direction oriented at right angles to the others. Figures walking on what appears to be a floor from one perspective are walking on walls or ceilings from another perspective. The illusion demonstrates how the visual system assigns depth and orientation relationships based on local cues such as surface texture, linear perspective, and the canonical upright orientation of human figures. Each gravitational plane maintains internal consistency with recognizable architectural elements and proper perspective, but the global configuration violates physical constraints by having three mutually perpendicular gravity fields intersect in shared spaces. The work exploits expectations about gravity's relationship to architectural structures and the human body's orientation. Viewers can follow individual figures through coherent paths within their own gravitational frame, but attempting to reconcile all three frames simultaneously reveals the impossibility. This lithograph represents one of Escher's most complex explorations of spatial paradox and has influenced discussions about relativity, perspective, and perceptual reference frames in both art and perceptual science.
