## Abstract

The missing square puzzle is a geometric dissection paradox in which a square or rectangular figure is divided into several polygonal pieces that are then rearranged to form an apparently identical shape with a different total area. The most common version involves a triangle divided into four pieces that can be reconfigured into a similar-looking triangle with an additional unit square of area mysteriously appearing or disappearing in the rearrangement. The resolution of the paradox lies in the fact that the hypotenuse of the reassembled triangle is not actually a straight line but consists of two line segments with slightly different slopes, creating a very thin parallelogram-shaped gap or overlap that accounts for the missing or extra area. The visual system's tendency to perceive nearly collinear edges as continuous straight lines causes observers to overlook this subtle geometric inconsistency, making the area discrepancy appear impossible. The missing square puzzle demonstrates how perceptual completion and grouping processes can lead to systematic misjudgments about geometric properties when local features are sufficiently ambiguous. It has been employed in mathematics education to illustrate the importance of rigorous geometric reasoning and the fallibility of perceptual intuition, while also providing insight into the mechanisms underlying contour interpolation and the perception of collinearity.
