## Abstract

The Blue Flash is an exceptionally rare atmospheric optical phenomenon closely related to the more commonly observed green flash, but involving the brief appearance of blue or violet light at the uppermost rim of the sun during sunrise or sunset. This phenomenon occurs under the same fundamental atmospheric conditions as the green flash—requiring atmospheric refraction that disperses sunlight into its component wavelengths—but represents an even more extreme manifestation of the effect. The blue flash appears when atmospheric conditions are extraordinarily clear and stable, allowing the shorter blue wavelengths to reach the observer's eye without being scattered away by atmospheric particles. Because blue light is scattered much more efficiently than green or red light by atmospheric molecules and aerosols, the blue flash requires near-perfect atmospheric transparency and is consequently observed far less frequently than its green counterpart. The duration of the blue flash is extremely brief, typically lasting only a fraction of a second, and occurs immediately after or before a green flash as the sun's disc crosses the horizon. Observations of genuine blue flashes are sufficiently rare that they remain a prized sighting among atmospheric optics enthusiasts and require exceptional viewing conditions including very clear air, a distant unobstructed horizon, and minimal atmospheric turbulence.
