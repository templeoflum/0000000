## Abstract

Kopfermann Cubes are a series of line drawings systematically created by Hertha Kopfermann in 1930 to investigate the conditions under which two-dimensional patterns are perceived as three-dimensional objects versus flat configurations. Kopfermann varied specific geometric properties such as angles, line lengths, and orientations to create a range of cube-like figures that differed in their tendency to evoke three-dimensional versus two-dimensional interpretations. Her research demonstrated that small changes in the stimulus configuration could dramatically alter whether observers perceive a drawing as representing a three-dimensional cube or merely as a flat pattern of lines in the picture plane. The figures illustrate fundamental principles of depth perception from two-dimensional images, including the importance of right angles appearing as right angles (not foreshortened), parallelism, symmetry, and familiarity with canonical viewpoints of three-dimensional objects. Kopfermann's work was influential in Gestalt psychology and contributed to understanding how the visual system uses regularities and simplicity principles to infer three-dimensional structure. Some configurations are strongly bistable, allowing observers to alternate between two-dimensional and three-dimensional interpretations, while others strongly favor one interpretation. The Kopfermann cube series has been used extensively in research on picture perception, depth ambiguity, and the relationship between two-dimensional image properties and three-dimensional scene interpretation.
