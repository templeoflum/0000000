## Abstract

The Motion Aftereffect (MAE), also known as the Waterfall Illusion, is one of the most robust and extensively studied phenomena in visual perception. First formally documented by Robert Addams in 1834 following his observation of stationary rocks appearing to move upward after prolonged viewing of a waterfall, the MAE occurs when a stationary stimulus appears to move in the direction opposite to previously viewed motion. The illusion demonstrates the adaptive nature of motion-sensitive neurons in the visual cortex, which become selectively fatigued during sustained exposure to unidirectional motion. When motion ceases, the imbalance in neural activity between direction-selective channels produces a compelling perception of movement in the null direction. The MAE has been documented across diverse stimulus types, viewing durations, and species, making it a fundamental tool for investigating motion processing mechanisms. The illusion exhibits parametric dependencies on adaptation duration, test stimulus characteristics, and retinal location, providing critical constraints for computational models of motion perception. Beyond its theoretical significance, the MAE serves as a diagnostic tool in clinical and developmental vision research.
