## Abstract

The face distortion effect, also known as flashed face distortion, is a striking phenomenon in which sequentially presented faces, when aligned at the eyes and presented in peripheral vision at rapid rates, appear grotesquely distorted with exaggerated or monstrous features. Normal faces that appear unremarkable when viewed individually or in central vision become perceptually warped when viewed under these specific presentation conditions. The effect requires peripheral viewing, alignment of facial features (particularly the eyes), and rapid alternation between different face images, typically at rates of 3-5 faces per second. The distortions manifest as extreme exaggerations of the distinctive features of each face relative to an implicitly computed average face representation. This phenomenon reveals the operation of face-specific adaptation mechanisms that calibrate perception relative to a running average of recently viewed faces, combined with the coarser spatial resolution and different processing characteristics of peripheral vision. The face distortion effect demonstrates that face perception involves continuous norm-based coding, where each face is encoded relative to a prototypical average, and that this adaptive coding operates even during rapid sequential presentation in peripheral vision. The phenomenon has become widely known through viral internet demonstrations.
