## Abstract

The Impossible Fork is an impossible object depicting a fork-like structure with three cylindrical prongs at one end that paradoxically transform into two prongs at the other end without any visible bifurcation or merging. The figure exploits ambiguities in how the visual system interprets figure-ground relationships and depth ordering in two-dimensional line drawings. At one end of the structure, three distinct circular cross-sections are clearly visible, suggesting three separate cylindrical tines. However, following these tines toward the opposite end reveals that the same structure appears to consist of only two rectangular bars separated by a single gap. This impossible transformation occurs because the middle region of the figure allows for multiple incompatible interpretations of which contours belong to solid surfaces versus which represent empty spaces between surfaces. The illusion demonstrates the constructive nature of depth perception and the visual system's reliance on local cues to infer three-dimensional structure. By carefully constructing regions where figure-ground assignment is ambiguous or contradictory, the figure creates a percept where different sections appear locally coherent but cannot be integrated into a globally consistent three-dimensional object. The Impossible Fork is closely related to the Impossible Trident or Blivet, and variations of this basic design appear in numerous contexts examining perceptual paradoxes. The figure serves as a powerful demonstration of how two-dimensional representations can exploit perceptual mechanisms to suggest impossible three-dimensional interpretations.
