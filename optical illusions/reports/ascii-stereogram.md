## Abstract

ASCII stereograms are text-based three-dimensional images created using standard ASCII characters arranged in side-by-side pairs that, when viewed with appropriate binocular fusion techniques such as cross-viewing or parallel viewing, produce vivid depth perception. These stereograms exploit binocular disparity, the fundamental cue underlying stereoscopic vision, by presenting slightly different character patterns to each eye. The horizontal displacement between corresponding characters in the left and right halves of the stereogram mimics the retinal disparity that normally arises from viewing three-dimensional objects from two slightly separated viewpoints. When the eyes converge at an appropriate distance to fuse the two halves, the visual system interprets the horizontal offsets as depth variations, creating the compelling perception of characters floating at different distances from the observer. ASCII stereograms emerged as an early digital art form and educational tool, requiring no special viewing equipment beyond the ability to control binocular vergence. The technique demonstrates that stereoscopic depth perception depends purely on horizontal disparity patterns rather than on realistic pictorial content. Contemporary examples range from simple geometric shapes to complex scenes encoded entirely in text characters. The phenomenon illustrates the visual system's remarkable ability to extract three-dimensional structure from minimal disparity information, even when that information is conveyed through the discrete spatial structure of text characters.
