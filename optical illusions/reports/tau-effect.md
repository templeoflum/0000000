## Abstract

The tau effect is a spatiotemporal illusion in which the perceived spatial distance between sequential events is systematically influenced by the temporal interval separating them. Specifically, when two events are separated by a longer temporal interval, the spatial distance between them is perceived as greater than when the same objective spatial separation occurs with a shorter temporal interval. This phenomenon represents the temporal-to-spatial counterpart of the kappa effect, which describes the influence of spatial separation on perceived temporal duration. Together, these illusions reveal bidirectional interactions between spatial and temporal perception, demonstrating that the brain integrates space and time according to implicit ecological regularities rather than processing them as independent dimensions. The tau effect has been documented across multiple sensory modalities including vision, touch, and audition, suggesting domain-general principles of spatiotemporal binding. Theoretical accounts typically invoke Bayesian inference frameworks in which the perceptual system incorporates prior expectations that events separated by longer time intervals are likely to be farther apart in space, reflecting regularities in how objects and events typically unfold in the physical world. The tau effect has implications for understanding multisensory integration, temporal perception, and the computational principles underlying perceptual organization.
