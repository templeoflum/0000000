## Abstract

The Occlusion Illusion describes a systematic bias in size perception whereby a partially occluded object appears larger than an identical fully visible object, despite both subtending the same retinal area in their visible portions. This phenomenon reflects the visual system's automatic tendency to complete surfaces behind occluders and to account for hidden portions when estimating object size. When an object is partially hidden by an occluding surface, perceptual mechanisms engage in amodal completion, mentally reconstructing the invisible portions based on visible contours and statistical regularities of natural scenes. This completion process can lead to size overestimation, as the visual system implicitly assumes additional extent beyond the visible region. Theoretical accounts invoke Bayesian inference frameworks, in which the visual system integrates retinal input with prior expectations about object continuity and typical occlusion configurations. The illusion demonstrates an adaptive bias, as objects in natural environments are frequently partially occluded, and successful interaction requires accurate estimation of complete object properties. Neural mechanisms involve mid-level visual areas that represent completed surfaces beyond early retinotopic coding. The Occlusion Illusion reveals how perception is fundamentally inferential, going beyond directly available sensory data to construct representations of the complete three-dimensional world, occasionally producing systematic errors in simplified laboratory displays.
