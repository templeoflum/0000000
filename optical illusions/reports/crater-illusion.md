## Abstract

The Crater Illusion, also known as the Concave/Convex Illusion, is a depth-from-shading phenomenon in which the same surface appears either as a protruding convex dome or a recessed concave crater depending on the assumed direction of illumination. This bistable percept arises from the visual system's learned prior assumption that illumination comes from above, a statistical regularity reflecting the position of the sun in natural environments. When viewing photographs of cratered surfaces such as lunar landscapes, craters with shadows on the bottom edge are typically perceived as depressions, while identical images rotated 180 degrees appear as raised bumps, because the shadow positions have been inverted relative to the assumed overhead light source. The illusion demonstrates that the brain actively infers three-dimensional surface structure from two-dimensional shading patterns by integrating shape-from-shading computations with strong top-down assumptions about environmental lighting geometry. The effect is remarkably robust, persisting even when observers are informed about the true surface structure or viewing geometry. Individual differences exist in the strength of the light-from-above prior, with some observers showing greater flexibility in perceiving alternative lighting interpretations. The Crater Illusion has important implications for understanding visual perception in unusual lighting environments, including underwater viewing, artificial illumination, and extraterrestrial exploration, where violated lighting assumptions can lead to systematic depth misjudgments.
