## Abstract

The Chevreul Illusion, described by French chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul in 1839, is a fundamental lightness contrast phenomenon in which adjacent bands of uniform luminance appear to have scalloped gradients, with enhanced contrast at their boundaries. When viewing a series of abutting stripes that each have uniform luminance but differ in luminance from their neighbors, observers perceive each band as being darker on the edge adjacent to a lighter band and lighter on the edge adjacent to a darker band. This creates an illusory impression of gradients within each physically uniform region, with a scalloped or Mach band-like appearance. The illusion is typically attributed to lateral inhibition mechanisms in early visual processing, whereby neurons responsive to a given retinal location inhibit neighboring neurons, leading to enhanced responses at luminance boundaries and reduced responses in uniform regions. This edge enhancement serves the functional purpose of increasing the salience of boundaries and transitions in the visual scene, facilitating object segmentation and contour detection. Chevreul's original observations were made in the context of textile design and color theory, predating formal understanding of retinal physiology by several decades. The illusion remains a classic demonstration of how simultaneous contrast effects and spatial filtering mechanisms shape lightness perception, illustrating the difference between physical stimulus properties and perceptual appearance.
