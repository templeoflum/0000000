## Abstract

The Stroop effect, discovered by John Ridley Stroop in 1935, is one of the most robust and widely studied phenomena in cognitive psychology, demonstrating the automaticity of reading and the difficulty of selective attention. The classic task requires participants to name the ink color of color words while ignoring the word's semantic meaning. When the word and ink color are incongruent (for example, the word "red" printed in green ink), reaction times are substantially slower and error rates higher compared to congruent trials (the word "red" in red ink) or neutral baselines. This interference effect reveals that reading is so highly automatized that word meaning is processed involuntarily, even when it is irrelevant to the task and detrimental to performance. The magnitude of the Stroop effect is modulated by factors including practice, attention, and individual differences in executive control capacity. Neuroimaging studies implicate anterior cingulate cortex in detecting response conflict and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex in implementing cognitive control to resolve interference. The Stroop task has become a standard measure of selective attention, cognitive control, and executive function in clinical, developmental, and individual differences research, with impaired Stroop performance associated with various neurological and psychiatric conditions including ADHD, schizophrenia, and frontal lobe damage.
