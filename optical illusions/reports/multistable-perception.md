## Abstract

Multistable perception refers to the general phenomenon where visual stimuli support three or more mutually exclusive perceptual interpretations that spontaneously alternate over time despite a constant physical input. Unlike bistable stimuli that alternate between two states, multistable displays permit multiple competing percepts, each temporarily dominating conscious awareness before giving way to another interpretation. This phenomenon reveals fundamental principles about how the visual system resolves perceptual ambiguity when sensory evidence equally supports multiple organizational schemas. Classic examples include ambiguous figures with multiple depth interpretations, rotating structures that can appear to move in various directions, and complex patterns supporting numerous figure-ground segmentations. The spontaneous alternations occur even when observers attempt to maintain a single interpretation, suggesting automatic neural competition processes that periodically destabilize dominant percepts. Research on multistable perception has provided critical insights into neural dynamics underlying conscious awareness, attentional control, and the constructive nature of visual experience. The phenomenon demonstrates that perception actively selects among possible interpretations rather than passively reflecting sensory input, with alternation rates influenced by factors including attention, prior knowledge, and individual cognitive styles. Understanding multistable perception remains central to theories of perceptual organization, consciousness, and neural dynamics.
