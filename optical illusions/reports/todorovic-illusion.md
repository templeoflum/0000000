## Abstract

The Todorovic Illusion demonstrates that the perceived lightness of target regions is determined more by the local junction structure and contour configuration around their edges than by the total area or luminance of the surrounding region. In this illusion, identical gray target squares appear different in lightness depending on whether they share extended contours with dark or light regions, even when the overall luminance of the surround is equated between conditions. The phenomenon was described by Dejan Todorovic and challenges theories that explain lightness solely in terms of spatial averaging, area ratios, or total surround luminance. Instead, the illusion reveals that the visual system gives special weight to T-junctions, X-junctions, and other edge configurations that provide information about surface layering and occlusion relationships. Targets that appear to be part of (or coplanar with) a particular surface region tend to assimilate toward that region's lightness, while targets separated by strong junctions show contrast effects. The Todorovic Illusion has been particularly influential in supporting anchoring theories and edge-classification approaches to lightness perception, which propose that the visual system segments the image into layers and computes lightness relative to local frameworks defined by junction geometry. The magnitude of the effect depends critically on the length and alignment of shared contours, demonstrating the importance of extended spatial structure in lightness computation.
