## Abstract

The Ouchi Illusion, discovered by Japanese artist and vision scientist Hajime Ouchi in 1977, produces a compelling perception of motion and depth from a purely static geometric pattern. The classic configuration consists of a central circular or elliptical region filled with horizontal line segments surrounded by a background of vertical line segments, or vice versa. When observers make small eye movements or shift their gaze across the pattern, the central disk appears to slide, float, or move independently from the background, creating an unstable perception of layered surfaces at different depths. The illusion does not require actual motion of the stimulus, though slight relative motion between the observer and display enhances the effect dramatically. The phenomenon arises from differential motion signals generated by orientation-selective neurons responding to retinal image shifts across regions of orthogonal texture. Fourier analysis reveals that the sharp orientation boundary creates spatial frequency components that preferentially activate motion-detection mechanisms during micro-saccades and pursuit eye movements. The Ouchi Illusion has inspired numerous artistic applications and serves as a valuable stimulus for investigating the interaction between form processing, motion detection, and depth perception in early visual cortex.
