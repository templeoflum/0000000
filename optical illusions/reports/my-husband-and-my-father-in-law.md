## Abstract

The "My Husband and My Father-in-Law" illusion is an ambiguous figure that can be perceived as either a young man's face or an older man's face, depending on how the visual features are organized by the observer's perceptual system. This classic bistable image demonstrates how identical contours and shading patterns can support two distinct facial interpretations through alternative figure-ground assignments and feature groupings. When perceived as the young man, certain contours define his jaw and ear, while the same contours form the older man's nose and eye region when the percept switches. The illusion exemplifies the constructive nature of face perception, showing that facial recognition involves active hypothesis-testing rather than passive feature detection. Spontaneous perceptual switches between the two interpretations occur even with sustained viewing, reflecting neural competition between alternative organizational schemas. The illusion has been valuable for studying the mechanisms underlying ambiguous figure perception, particularly how prior expectations, attentional focus, and semantic knowledge influence which interpretation dominates at any moment. Research using this and similar family-relationship illusions has illuminated the role of top-down processing in face perception and provided paradigms for investigating the neural dynamics of perceptual bistability.
