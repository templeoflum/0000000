## Abstract

The Dynamic Illusory Size-Contrast Effect, commonly abbreviated as the DISC Effect, is a motion-induced size illusion in which dynamic changes in the surrounding visual context produce systematic misperceptions of target size. When a target stimulus is presented against a background of expanding or contracting elements, the target appears to change in size in the direction opposite to the context motion, even when the target itself remains physically constant in size. For example, a circle surrounded by an expanding pattern appears to shrink, while the same circle surrounded by a contracting pattern appears to grow. The magnitude of the illusory size change is proportional to the velocity and spatial extent of the background motion, with stronger contextual motion producing more pronounced size distortions. The effect demonstrates powerful interactions between motion processing and size perception, revealing that dynamic visual information influences not only motion percepts but also the perceived spatial properties of objects. The DISC effect has been attributed to motion-based predictive coding mechanisms, where the visual system uses motion signals to anticipate future states and adjust current perceptual representations accordingly. It may also involve spatial interactions between motion-sensitive mechanisms and size-coding neurons. The phenomenon provides evidence for the deeply integrated nature of visual processing, where traditionally separate attributes such as motion and size influence one another through shared neural mechanisms.
