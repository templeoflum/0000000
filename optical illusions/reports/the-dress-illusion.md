## Abstract

The Dress illusion emerged in February 2015 when a photograph of a striped dress went viral on social media, revealing a striking and persistent individual difference in color perception. Approximately 57% of observers perceive the dress as white with gold stripes, while 30% see it as blue with black stripes, with the remaining observers experiencing intermediate or alternating percepts. The same physical image produces radically different color experiences across individuals, with each observer typically experiencing their percept as stable and compelling. The phenomenon arises from ambiguous illumination cues in the photograph: the dress was actually blue and black, but overexposure and color temperature ambiguity create uncertainty about whether the scene is illuminated by warm (yellowish) or cool (bluish) light. Color constancy mechanisms discount assumed illumination to recover surface reflectance, but different observers make opposite assumptions about the lighting, leading to divergent percepts. Research suggests that individual differences correlate with chronotype, visual environment statistics, and habitual assumptions about natural lighting. The Dress illusion has become a landmark demonstration that identical retinal stimulation can produce categorically different conscious experiences, providing unique opportunities to study individual differences in perceptual inference and the neural basis of subjective color experience.
