## Abstract

Bloch's Law describes a fundamental principle of temporal integration in visual perception: within a critical duration, the visual system integrates luminance over time such that perceived brightness depends on the total energy (luminance multiplied by duration) rather than either parameter alone. Formally, I × t = k, where I is stimulus intensity, t is duration, and k is a constant threshold. This means that a brief, intense flash can appear equally bright as a longer, dimmer presentation if their products are equal. The critical duration varies with retinal location, adaptation state, and stimulus parameters but typically ranges from 50 to 100 milliseconds for foveal vision. Beyond this critical duration, Bloch's Law breaks down and brightness perception becomes increasingly dependent on intensity alone. The law reflects the temporal integration properties of photoreceptors and early visual processing stages, which pool signals over brief timescales to improve signal-to-noise ratios for detection. Bloch's Law has important implications for understanding flicker fusion, apparent motion, masking phenomena, and threshold detection tasks. It demonstrates that the visual system operates as a leaky temporal integrator with limited temporal resolution, trading temporal precision for sensitivity enhancement.
