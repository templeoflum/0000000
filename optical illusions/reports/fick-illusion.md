## Abstract

The Fick Illusion is a geometric size illusion in which a vertical line appears longer than a horizontal line of equal physical length when presented in an inverted-T configuration. First described by German physiologist Adolf Eugen Fick in 1851, this illusion represents one of the earliest documented anisotropies in spatial perception. The effect is robust across viewing conditions and demonstrates a systematic bias in the perception of vertical versus horizontal extent. The illusion is closely related to the vertical-horizontal illusion and shares similar underlying mechanisms. Theoretical accounts typically invoke orientation-selective processing in early visual cortex, where neural resources may be differentially allocated to vertical and horizontal orientations. Alternative explanations include scanning eye movement patterns that traverse vertical extents differently than horizontal ones, or misapplied size constancy scaling that treats vertical extents as if they represent depth in the visual field. The Fick Illusion has significance for understanding fundamental anisotropies in spatial vision and has practical implications for graphic design, typography, and architectural perception where vertical and horizontal elements must be perceptually balanced.
