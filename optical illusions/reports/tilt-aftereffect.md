## Abstract

The tilt aftereffect, first systematically investigated by James J. Gibson and Mowbray Radner in 1937, is a fundamental orientation adaptation phenomenon in which prolonged viewing of lines or gratings tilted in one direction causes subsequently viewed vertical or near-vertical lines to appear tilted in the opposite direction. For example, after adapting to lines tilted 15 degrees clockwise from vertical, a truly vertical test line appears tilted counterclockwise. The effect is maximal for adaptation angles of approximately 10-20 degrees from the test orientation and exhibits characteristic tuning properties that reflect the underlying neural mechanisms. The tilt aftereffect demonstrates the existence of orientation-selective neural populations in early visual cortex (V1) that adapt with prolonged stimulation, shifting the population response pattern and causing repulsive perceptual biases. The phenomenon exhibits strong retinotopic specificity, occurring primarily at the adapted retinal location, though some interocular transfer indicates binocular cortical involvement. The tilt aftereffect has been one of the most extensively studied visual aftereffects and has played a crucial role in establishing the psychophysical and neural basis of orientation processing. It provided early evidence linking perceptual phenomena to the orientation-selective properties of cortical neurons discovered by Hubel and Wiesel.
