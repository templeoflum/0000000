## Abstract

The Line Motion Illusion, first systematically described by Odmar Neumann and Werner Klotz in 1994, occurs when a horizontal or vertical line is presented simultaneously along its entire length, but appears to grow or extend progressively from one end to the other. The key manipulation involves presenting a brief visual cue near one end of the line immediately before the line appears. When observers view this sequence, they report that the line seems to be drawn or painted from the cued end toward the opposite end, despite the fact that all segments of the line are physically present simultaneously. The illusory motion typically proceeds smoothly and continuously from the cued location, creating a compelling impression of progressive construction or unfolding. The strength and reliability of the illusion depend on the temporal interval between cue and line onset, the spatial proximity of the cue to the line, and attentional factors. The Line Motion Illusion demonstrates that spatial attention, when drawn to a specific location by a cue, can fundamentally alter the temporal perception of stimulus onset across space. The phenomenon has been interpreted as reflecting attentional facilitation of processing at the cued location, creating an asynchrony in the perceptual registration of simultaneously presented elements. Understanding this illusion provides insights into the interaction between attention, temporal processing, and motion perception.
