## Abstract

The Rorschach Inkblots are a set of symmetrical, ambiguous inkblot images developed by Swiss psychiatrist Hermann Rorschach in 1921, originally designed for personality assessment but representing a powerful demonstration of how observers impose meaningful interpretations on inherently ambiguous stimuli. Unlike other bistable illusions that alternate between a small number of specific percepts, Rorschach inkblots support virtually unlimited interpretations, with different observers perceiving different objects, scenes, or figures based on their individual cognitive styles, expectations, and past experiences. The bilateral symmetry of the inkblots provides organizational structure while leaving semantic content radically underdetermined, making them maximally projective stimuli. Observers spontaneously organize the irregular contours, shading variations, and color patches into coherent representations of animals, human figures, landscapes, or abstract concepts through a process of active perceptual inference. The phenomenon demonstrates the constructive nature of perception, showing that meaningful visual experience emerges from the interaction between sensory input and top-down interpretive processes. While the clinical utility of Rorschach testing remains controversial, the inkblots have proven valuable for studying individual differences in perceptual organization, the role of semantic knowledge in pattern recognition, and how ambiguous stimuli are resolved through projection of internal mental models onto external sensory data.
