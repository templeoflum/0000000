## Abstract

The Hyperboloid Illusion is a geometric depth phenomenon in which straight rods or lines arranged in a circular configuration with systematic rotation appear to form a curved hyperboloid surface rather than remaining in a flat plane or simple cylindrical arrangement. This illusion demonstrates how the visual system integrates local orientation information across space to construct global three-dimensional surface percepts. When viewing the display, observers perceive a smoothly curved saddle-shaped or hourglass-shaped surface despite the physical straightness of individual elements. The effect arises from contour integration mechanisms that interpolate surface structure from the orientation field defined by the rod arrangement, combined with Bayesian inference processes that favor smooth surface interpretations. The illusion is enhanced when rods are presented with appropriate perspective cues or when the display rotates, providing motion parallax information consistent with a curved surface. The phenomenon illustrates fundamental principles of mid-level vision, including the integration of local edge and orientation measurements into global surface representations, the role of smoothness priors in shape perception, and the visual system's tendency to perceive continuous surfaces even from discrete orientation samples. The Hyperboloid Illusion has architectural and sculptural applications, where straight structural elements can create the compelling appearance of complex curved surfaces, and serves as a valuable tool for investigating the computational mechanisms underlying surface perception from orientation fields.
