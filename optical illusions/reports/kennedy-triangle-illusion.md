## Abstract

The Kennedy Triangle Illusion is a geometric illusion involving triangular configurations that produce systematic misperceptions of size, angle, or spatial relationships. While the specific historical provenance and discoverer of this illusion remain uncertain, it represents one of several triangle-based illusions that have been studied in the perceptual literature. Geometric illusions involving triangles often exploit the visual system's sensitivity to angles, vertex configurations, and the spatial relationships among multiple elements. Such illusions may involve perceived distortions of triangle height, base length, internal angles, or the apparent equality of different triangular shapes. The underlying mechanisms likely involve early spatial filtering operations and contour integration processes in primary visual cortex, where local edge orientations and vertex features are encoded. Contextual influences from surrounding elements can bias these representations, leading to systematic errors in perceived geometry. Triangle-based illusions have theoretical importance for understanding how the visual system constructs representations of planar shapes and integrates local edge information into global form percepts. Further research is needed to clarify the precise stimulus parameters, perceptual effects, and theoretical mechanisms specific to the Kennedy Triangle Illusion, as well as to establish its relationship to other geometric illusions involving polygonal shapes.
