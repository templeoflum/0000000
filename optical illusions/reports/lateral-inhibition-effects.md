## Abstract

Lateral inhibition effects encompass a family of perceptual phenomena that arise from the inhibitory interactions between neighboring neurons in sensory systems, particularly in the visual pathway. This neural mechanism, in which active neurons suppress the activity of adjacent neurons, serves to enhance contrast, sharpen spatial boundaries, and improve edge detection by emphasizing differences in stimulation between neighboring regions. Classic manifestations include Mach bands, where illusory bright and dark bands appear at luminance gradients; the Hermann grid illusion, where dark spots appear at intersections of white bars on a black background; and simultaneous brightness contrast, where the perceived brightness of a region depends on the luminance of surrounding areas. Lateral inhibition operates at multiple levels of visual processing, from retinal circuitry involving horizontal and amacrine cells to cortical interactions in V1 and beyond. The spatial extent and strength of inhibition varies across the visual field, being stronger in central vision than periphery. While lateral inhibition provides a parsimonious explanation for many early visual phenomena, more complex contextual effects may involve additional mechanisms including cortical feedback, normalization, and higher-level surface representation. Individual differences in lateral inhibition strength may relate to variations in perceptual sensitivity and clinical conditions.
