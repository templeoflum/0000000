## Abstract

The crowding effect describes the impairment in identifying a peripheral target stimulus when it is flanked by nearby objects, even when the target itself is well above detection threshold. This phenomenon represents a fundamental bottleneck in peripheral object recognition that cannot be explained by simple acuity limitations. Crowding is characterized by a critical spacing that scales with eccentricity, typically requiring flankers to be separated by at least half the target's eccentricity for accurate identification. Within this critical zone, the target and flankers appear jumbled or feature-swapped rather than simply blurred. The effect is highly dependent on the similarity between target and flankers, with greater interference from similar features. Crowding is distinctly asymmetric, being stronger on the foveal side of the target than the peripheral side, and stronger in the radial than tangential direction. Unlike masking, crowding does not require temporal overlap and cannot be overcome by increased contrast. The phenomenon constrains reading speed and object recognition in peripheral vision, has implications for visual search and scene perception, and may reflect obligatory integration of features within pooling regions of visual cortex.
