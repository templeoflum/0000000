## Abstract

Change blindness refers to the striking failure to detect substantial changes in a visual scene when those changes occur during a visual disruption such as a saccade, blink, brief blank screen, or other transient. Despite the often dramatic nature of the changes, which can include objects appearing, disappearing, changing color, or changing location, observers frequently remain unaware of these alterations even after multiple presentations. This phenomenon challenges intuitive assumptions about the completeness and stability of visual representation, suggesting that detailed scene information is not stored across disruptions as comprehensively as subjective experience implies. Change blindness has been demonstrated across numerous paradigms including flicker tasks, continuity errors in films, and real-world interactions where experimenters switch identities mid-conversation. The effect reveals that focused attention is necessary for explicit change detection and that much of our sense of visual richness derives from the availability of information in the world rather than internal storage. Individual differences in change detection correlate with attentional capacity and working memory. The phenomenon has significant implications for eyewitness testimony, user interface design, and understanding normal visual awareness.
