## Abstract

The Ponzo illusion is a classic size-contrast illusion in which two identical horizontal lines placed between converging lines appear to be different lengths, with the upper line appearing substantially longer than the lower line. First reported by Italian psychologist Mario Ponzo in 1911, the illusion is typically attributed to the misapplication of linear perspective depth cues. The converging lines mimic the appearance of parallel lines receding into the distance, such as railroad tracks or a road vanishing toward the horizon. The visual system interprets the upper line as being farther away in depth, and consequently applies size constancy scaling, expanding its perceived size to compensate for its presumed greater distance. This explanation positions the Ponzo illusion as a prime example of how pictorial depth cues can override veridical size perception even in flat, two-dimensional stimuli. The illusion magnitude varies with the angle of convergence and the position of the test lines along the converging context. Beyond its theoretical importance for understanding size constancy mechanisms, the Ponzo illusion has been used extensively in developmental psychology, cross-cultural perception studies, and as a clinical tool for assessing visual processing abnormalities in neurological and psychiatric populations.
