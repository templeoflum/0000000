## Abstract

Floor illusions are painted or printed patterns applied to horizontal surfaces that create compelling perceptual experiences of three-dimensional depth, including apparent holes, chasms, raised platforms, or distorted surfaces, despite being physically flat. These artistic installations exploit the visual system's depth perception mechanisms by manipulating pictorial depth cues such as linear perspective, texture gradients, shading, and occlusion to generate interpretations that conflict with the actual planar geometry of the surface. The illusions are particularly effective because floors are typically viewed from oblique angles where perspective foreshortening naturally occurs, allowing artists to create images that match the expected retinal projections of three-dimensional structures. Floor illusions demonstrate the dominance of monocular depth cues in situations where binocular disparity and motion parallax information may be weak or ambiguous, as well as the visual system's application of Bayesian inference to interpret visual input as the most probable three-dimensional configuration. The phenomenon reveals how contextual expectations about floor surfaces can be violated to create experiences ranging from mild spatial distortion to compelling illusions that trigger behavioral responses such as hesitation or altered gait patterns. Contemporary applications include museum installations, commercial advertising, safety awareness campaigns, and public art projects that engage viewers through interactive perceptual experiences.
