## Abstract

The Poggendorff illusion is a geometric illusion in which a diagonal line passing behind an occluding vertical rectangle appears misaligned with its continuation on the other side, despite being collinear. First described by Johann Christian Poggendorff in 1860, the illusion demonstrates systematic errors in extrapolating contour trajectories across occluded regions. The magnitude of misalignment depends on the angle of the transversal line relative to the occluding edges, with maximum effect occurring at intermediate angles (typically 20-70 degrees from vertical). Theoretical accounts invoke several mechanisms: acute angle expansion, in which angles between the transversal and parallels appear larger than they are; depth interpretation, where the occluder is perceived as closer in space, inducing inappropriate size constancy scaling; and contour integration failures in bridging discontinuous line segments. The illusion has been extensively studied as a test case for understanding how the visual system completes partially occluded contours and has implications for theories of spatial integration and perceptual completion. The Poggendorff illusion remains significant in perceptual research as it reveals fundamental constraints on how visual pathways extrapolate continuous features across interrupted regions.
