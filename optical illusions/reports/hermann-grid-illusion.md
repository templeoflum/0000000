## Abstract

The Hermann Grid Illusion, first described by Ludimar Hermann in 1870, is one of the classic demonstrations of lateral inhibition in the visual system. The illusion consists of a regular grid of black squares separated by white corridors. When viewing this configuration, observers perceive dark or ghostly gray spots at the intersections of the white corridors, despite these intersection regions being physically identical in luminance to the corridor segments. Critically, these illusory spots tend to disappear when directly fixated, appearing primarily in peripheral vision. The traditional explanation, proposed by Baumgartner in 1960, invokes lateral inhibition in retinal ganglion cells with concentric center-surround receptive fields. At corridor intersections, a receptive field centered on the white region receives more lateral inhibition from surrounding white areas compared to positions along the corridors, resulting in reduced neural response and the perception of darkening. However, this classical account has been challenged by demonstrations that certain variations of the grid, such as curved or wavy grids, eliminate the illusion despite preserving the local luminance relationships. Contemporary accounts invoke spatial filtering at multiple scales and orientation-selective mechanisms in cortical processing. The Hermann Grid remains an important test case for theories of early spatial vision and continues to generate debate about the neural mechanisms underlying lightness perception.
