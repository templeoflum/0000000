## Abstract

The Chromatic Motion Illusion refers to illusory motion percepts that arise from purely chromatic modulation in the absence of luminance contrast, or conversely, the distinctive characteristics of motion perception when stimuli are defined solely by chromatic differences. At equiluminance (isoluminance), where stimuli differ only in chromaticity but not in brightness, the human visual system exhibits severely degraded motion sensitivity compared to luminance-defined motion. However, under certain conditions, purely chromatic stimuli can still produce compelling motion percepts, albeit with slower apparent speeds and coarser spatial resolution. Additionally, some configurations of chromatic patterns produce illusory drifting or flowing motions despite being statically presented, suggesting interactions between chromatic processing and motion detection pathways. The phenomenon reveals a fundamental dissociation in how the visual system processes motion information through parallel luminance-sensitive (magnocellular-dominated) and color-sensitive (parvocellular-dominated) pathways. While luminance-defined motion is processed rapidly and at high temporal frequencies, chromatic motion relies on slower mechanisms with lower temporal resolution. The illusion has important implications for understanding the neural architecture of visual processing and has been used to isolate contributions of different retinogeniculate pathways to motion perception. It also informs design considerations for displays and visual interfaces where chromatic and luminance information may vary independently.
