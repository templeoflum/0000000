## Abstract

Mach's Figure, created by physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach, is a classic depth-ambiguous stimulus depicting a book-like or folded-paper configuration that spontaneously reverses between two three-dimensional interpretations: appearing either as opening toward the observer or opening away from the observer. The figure consists of two rectangular surfaces meeting at a central fold or hinge, rendered with shading gradients that are consistent with both interpretations. This ambiguity arises because the same pattern of light and shadow can result from either a convex fold (mountain fold) with surfaces angling toward the viewer or a concave fold (valley fold) with surfaces angling away. The illusion demonstrates the fundamental role of assumptions about lighting direction and surface orientation in depth perception. Observers experience involuntary perceptual reversals between the two interpretations, similar to other bistable depth figures like the Necker cube. Mach used this figure to illustrate his philosophical views on the relationship between sensations and physical reality, arguing that perception involves unconscious inference rather than direct access to objective properties of the world. The figure has been influential in research on depth perception, perceptual organization, and the neural mechanisms underlying bistable perception. It exemplifies how the same retinal image can support multiple three-dimensional interpretations and how the brain actively constructs perceptual experience from ambiguous sensory input.
