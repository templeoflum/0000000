## Abstract

The Impossible Cylinder is an impossible object that depicts a cylindrical structure with contradictory cross-sections that cannot coexist in three-dimensional space. The figure typically shows a cylinder or tube where one end appears circular when viewed from a particular orientation while the other end appears rectangular or otherwise non-circular, despite the cylinder maintaining apparently straight and continuous sides connecting these incompatible endpoints. This creates a paradoxical percept where the same three-dimensional object appears to smoothly transition from one geometric form to an incompatible geometric form without any visible distortion or tapering of its lateral surfaces. The illusion exploits depth cue conflicts and the visual system's tendency to interpret two-dimensional contours as projections of three-dimensional objects with regular geometric properties. By carefully controlling visible edges, occlusion relationships, and surface boundaries, the figure presents local regions that each appear consistent with normal cylindrical geometry while the global configuration violates fundamental topological constraints. Recent physical realizations of such objects using carefully designed three-dimensional structures demonstrate that viewing-angle-dependent shapes can approximate the impossible percept when seen from specific vantage points, though these constructions necessarily involve hidden distortions that become apparent from other viewpoints. The Impossible Cylinder exemplifies how perceptual interpretations of depth and form depend critically on the available visual information and the assumptions the visual system makes about object regularity.
