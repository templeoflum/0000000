## Abstract

Piaget's Rectangle Illusion refers to geometric misperceptions studied by Swiss developmental psychologist Jean Piaget as part of his extensive investigations into the ontogeny of perceptual and cognitive abilities in children. Piaget examined how children of different ages perceive spatial relationships, proportions, and geometric properties, documenting systematic biases in size, length, and area judgments. The rectangle illusion specifically involves configurations where rectangles of equal area but different aspect ratios are judged as having different sizes, or where rectangles in different orientations or contextual arrangements produce biased perceptual comparisons. Piaget's developmental approach revealed that susceptibility to geometric illusions changes with age, with some illusions showing decreased magnitude as children mature and perceptual constancy mechanisms develop, while others remain stable across the lifespan. His work emphasized that perception involves active construction rather than passive registration of sensory data, and that this constructive process undergoes systematic development. The illusion is significant for understanding how perceptual organization principles emerge and stabilize during childhood, and how cognitive factors increasingly modulate perceptual interpretation. Contemporary research has built on Piaget's foundational observations using more controlled psychophysical methods, confirming developmental trajectories in geometric perception and relating them to neural maturation of visual pathways and higher-order cognitive systems involved in spatial reasoning.
