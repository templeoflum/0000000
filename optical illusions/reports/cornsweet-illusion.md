## Abstract

The Cornsweet Illusion, described by Tom Cornsweet in 1970 and independently discovered by Kenneth Craik and Vivian O'Brien, is a compelling lightness illusion in which two regions of identical uniform luminance appear markedly different in brightness when separated by a specialized edge profile called a Cornsweet edge. The Cornsweet edge consists of a sharp luminance cusp or step at the boundary between two regions, accompanied by shallow gradients that extend into each region before reaching a uniform plateau. Critically, the luminance values of the two plateau regions are physically identical, yet the region with the darker gradient near the edge appears substantially darker overall, while the region with the lighter gradient appears lighter overall. When the edge is occluded or removed, the illusion disappears and the two regions are perceived veridically as identical. This phenomenon demonstrates that lightness perception is dominated by edge information rather than by the average or absolute luminance of extended regions. The illusion is typically explained through spatial filtering models in which the visual system extracts and amplifies edge information while attenuating low spatial frequency information corresponding to gradual variations. The Cornsweet Illusion has been influential in developing theories of lightness perception that emphasize edge integration and filling-in processes, whereby surface lightness is computed by integrating along boundaries rather than averaging local luminance values.
