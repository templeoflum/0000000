## Abstract

The Brentano Illusion is a geometric distortion phenomenon closely related to the Müller-Lyer illusion, named after philosopher and psychologist Franz Brentano. In this configuration, two line segments share a common vertex, forming an angle similar to the classic Müller-Lyer arrowhead arrangement, but the task involves judging the location of the bisection point rather than overall line length. Observers systematically misperceive where the vertex divides the composite figure, with the apparent midpoint shifted away from its true geometric position. This displacement demonstrates how the orientation of flanking elements influences not only length perception but also spatial localization judgments. The illusion provides evidence that the same mechanisms underlying Müller-Lyer effects extend to positional coding in the visual system. Theoretical accounts emphasize how orientation-selective mechanisms and inappropriate application of depth or perspective cues can bias the representation of spatial locations. The Brentano configuration has been used to dissociate different aspects of spatial processing, revealing that misperception of position and misperception of extent may reflect partially distinct computational processes. Psychophysical studies have quantified the magnitude of positional shifts as a function of angle size and line length, contributing to broader understanding of how local features influence global spatial judgments.
