## Abstract

The Monroe-Einstein Illusion is a hybrid image that demonstrates distance-dependent face perception through spatial frequency filtering. When viewed up close, the image appears as Albert Einstein, but when viewed from a distance or with blurred vision, it transforms into an image of Marilyn Monroe. This striking illusion is created by combining the high spatial frequency components of one face with the low spatial frequency components of another face in a single composite image. The effect exploits fundamental properties of spatial vision: at close viewing distances, the visual system has access to fine detail (high spatial frequencies), while at far distances or with defocused vision, only coarse information (low spatial frequencies) is available. The illusion demonstrates that face recognition can operate on different scales of spatial information, with different faces being more recognizable at different spatial frequency bands. This phenomenon relates to coarse-to-fine processing theories in vision, which propose that visual analysis proceeds hierarchically from low spatial frequencies carrying global form information to high spatial frequencies carrying local detail. The Monroe-Einstein illusion has become a popular demonstration in vision science education and has been used to study the role of spatial frequency channels in face perception, the neural mechanisms of face recognition, and individual differences in visual processing strategies.
