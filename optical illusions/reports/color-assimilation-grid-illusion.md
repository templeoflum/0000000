## Abstract

The Color Assimilation Grid Illusion is a striking chromatic phenomenon in which a grayscale image overlaid with a colored grid of fine lines appears to contain the full spectrum of grid colors, despite the underlying image containing no chromatic information. The illusion became widely known through viral social media demonstrations showing black-and-white photographs that appear fully colored when viewed through a sparse colored grid overlay. The effect is strongest when the grid lines are thin, the spacing is optimized relative to viewing distance, and the colored lines align with or follow the contours of objects in the image. The phenomenon represents a powerful example of color assimilation (also called the spreading effect), where colors from small spatially interspersed elements spread perceptually into adjacent achromatic regions. Multiple mechanisms likely contribute: spatial filtering in early vision causes physical blurring that mixes chromatic and achromatic signals; lateral interactions in retinal and cortical color-opponent channels promote assimilation at high spatial frequencies; and higher-level filling-in processes may extrapolate color across boundaries based on contextual expectations. The illusion demonstrates the constructive nature of color perception and has generated renewed interest in classical assimilation phenomena. It also has implications for understanding how the visual system integrates sparse color information and for applications in computational colorization and image compression.
