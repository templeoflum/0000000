## Abstract

Contingent aftereffects represent a sophisticated class of visual adaptation phenomena in which the perceptual aftereffect is dependent on the simultaneous presence of two or more stimulus dimensions. Unlike simple aftereffects that occur along a single perceptual dimension, contingent aftereffects demonstrate that the visual system can adapt to specific conjunctions of features, revealing the multidimensional nature of neural coding in visual cortex. The most famous example is the McCollough effect, where adaptation to colored gratings of different orientations produces color aftereffects that are contingent on orientation. Other documented contingent aftereffects include motion-contingent color aftereffects, size-contingent tilt aftereffects, and spatial-frequency-contingent orientation aftereffects. These phenomena suggest that adaptation occurs at sites in the visual pathway where multiple feature dimensions are jointly encoded, likely in populations of neurons that are simultaneously selective for multiple stimulus attributes. Contingent aftereffects can persist for extraordinarily long durations, sometimes lasting days or weeks, distinguishing them from simple sensory adaptation. They have provided important insights into neural coding strategies, perceptual learning mechanisms, and the hierarchical organization of visual processing.
