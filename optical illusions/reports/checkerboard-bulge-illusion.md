## Abstract

The Checkerboard Bulge Illusion is a geometric distortion effect in which a regular checkerboard pattern with strategically displaced edges appears to bulge outward from the plane, creating a three-dimensional convex appearance despite being presented on a flat surface. The illusion typically involves systematic shifts in the position of individual square edges or boundaries, creating local perturbations in an otherwise regular grid. These displacements are carefully calibrated to produce coherent perception of volumetric expansion, with the center of the pattern appearing to project toward the observer. The phenomenon demonstrates how the visual system uses local edge relationships and spatial gradients to infer surface shape and three-dimensional structure. Theoretical accounts emphasize spatial frequency filtering mechanisms that respond to the pattern of edge displacements, along with mid-level contour integration processes that interpret these local signals as evidence for curved surfaces. The illusion reveals constraints on shape-from-contour computations and shows how systematic violations of regularity in periodic patterns can drive strong depth percepts. Experimental manipulations of displacement magnitude, spatial frequency of the checkerboard, and viewing distance have revealed parametric relationships between stimulus properties and the strength of the perceived bulge, providing insights into the spatial scale and computational mechanisms involved in deriving shape from two-dimensional contour information.
