## Abstract

The Ternus Effect, discovered by Josef Ternus in 1926, is a classic apparent motion phenomenon that demonstrates two qualitatively distinct types of motion perception from sequential displays of dot arrays. The standard Ternus display consists of three aligned dots presented in two alternating frames: the first frame shows dots at positions 1, 2, and 3, while the second frame shows dots at positions 2, 3, and 4. Depending on the interstimulus interval between frames, observers perceive either element motion, in which the outer dots appear to move individually while the central dots remain stationary, or group motion, in which all three dots appear to move together as a unified group. The transition from element motion to group motion occurs as the interstimulus interval increases beyond approximately 40-60 milliseconds. The Ternus Effect reveals that apparent motion is not a simple matter of matching nearest neighbors across frames, but rather involves complex processes that can segment or group elements based on spatiotemporal relationships. The phenomenon has been influential in theories of motion correspondence, figure-ground segmentation, and the interaction between low-level motion detection and high-level perceptual organization.
