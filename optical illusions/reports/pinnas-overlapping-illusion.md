## Abstract

Pinna's Overlapping Illusion is one of several geometric and chromatic illusions discovered by Italian perceptual psychologist Baingio Pinna, who has contributed extensively to the catalog of visual phenomena involving color spreading, shape perception, and spatial interactions. The overlapping illusion specifically involves configurations where multiple shapes or regions overlap in particular ways, producing systematic distortions in perceived size, position, or spatial relationships. When shapes partially occlude one another with specific edge configurations and contrast relationships, observers may misperceive the relative sizes of the overlapping regions, or may experience illusory shifts in the perceived positions of boundaries. The phenomenon is related to broader principles of figure-ground segregation and border ownership assignment, processes by which the visual system determines which surfaces own shared contours and how depth ordering is represented. Theoretical accounts emphasize the role of mid-level vision mechanisms that integrate local edge information into coherent surface representations, and how these integration processes can be systematically biased by particular stimulus configurations. Pinna's work has demonstrated that relatively simple manipulations of local features can produce dramatic effects on global percepts, revealing the complex interplay between bottom-up feature detection and top-down organizational principles. The illusion contributes to understanding how the visual system resolves inherent ambiguities in two-dimensional retinal images to construct stable three-dimensional scene interpretations.
