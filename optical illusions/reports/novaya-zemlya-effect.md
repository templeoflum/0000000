## Abstract

The Novaya Zemlya effect is a rare and extreme atmospheric optical phenomenon in which the sun appears to rise significantly earlier than astronomically predicted, or remains visible after it should have geometrically set, due to extreme atmospheric refraction in polar temperature inversion conditions. Named after observations made during Willem Barents's 1596-1597 Arctic expedition to Novaya Zemlya, where the sun was observed to rise approximately two weeks earlier than geometric calculations indicated, this effect results from strong atmospheric ducting in which light is confined to and guided through a layer of air with specific thermal structure. Unlike ordinary atmospheric refraction which typically advances apparent sunrise or delays apparent sunset by only a few minutes, the Novaya Zemlya effect can create discrepancies of days or weeks under extreme polar conditions. The phenomenon requires an unusually strong and stable temperature inversion with warmer air sandwiched between colder layers, creating a waveguide for light rays that would normally pass over the observer's location. Light becomes trapped in this layer through repeated refractive bending, allowing it to follow the curvature of the Earth far beyond what standard atmospheric refraction permits. This effect represents an extreme manifestation of atmospheric optical ducting and demonstrates the profound influence that atmospheric thermal structure can have on light propagation and astronomical observations in polar regions.
