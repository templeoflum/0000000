## Abstract

The Chromatic Adaptation Illusion encompasses a family of phenomena in which prolonged viewing of a chromatically biased stimulus causes systematic shifts in the perceived color of subsequently viewed stimuli. Following adaptation to a colored field, neutral gray surfaces appear tinted with the complementary color, and colored surfaces undergo predictable shifts in hue and saturation. The magnitude and time course of these shifts depend on the intensity, duration, and spectral composition of the adapting stimulus, as well as the spatial and temporal relationship between adapter and test stimuli. Chromatic adaptation serves an important functional role in color constancy, allowing the visual system to discount changes in ambient illumination and maintain stable color percepts across diverse lighting environments. However, the same mechanisms produce illusory color shifts when the adaptation is spatially or temporally localized. The phenomenon arises from gain control mechanisms operating at multiple stages of visual processing, including photoreceptor desensitization in the retina and opponent-channel recalibration in cortex. Chromatic adaptation effects have been extensively studied using both psychophysical and neurophysiological methods, providing insights into the neural basis of color perception and constancy. The illusion demonstrates that color appearance is fundamentally context-dependent and dynamically regulated rather than a simple readout of wavelength composition.
