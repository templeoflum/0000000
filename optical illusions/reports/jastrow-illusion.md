## Abstract

The Jastrow Illusion is a robust size illusion in which two identical curvilinear shapes—typically annular sectors or fan-shaped figures with one curved edge longer than the other—appear markedly different in size when arranged such that the longer edge of the upper shape is adjacent to the shorter edge of the lower shape. Discovered by Polish-American psychologist Joseph Jastrow in 1889, the illusion demonstrates that perceived size depends critically on local spatial relationships and edge comparisons rather than global shape metrics. When the two identical shapes are presented in this configuration, the lower shape consistently appears substantially larger than the upper one, even though they are physically identical. The effect is so compelling that observers often express surprise when the shapes are superimposed or measured. The illusion arises from the visual system's tendency to compare adjacent contours: the short edge of the top shape is compared with the long edge of the bottom shape at their point of proximity, biasing the entire shape's perceived size. This demonstrates the importance of local edge relationships in size perception and highlights how spatial context influences global object properties. The Jastrow Illusion has been extensively used in perceptual research and has inspired variations using different curvilinear shapes, all demonstrating similar context-dependent size misperceptions.
