## Abstract

The Mona Lisa Illusion, also known as the gaze-following effect, refers to the perceptual phenomenon in which the eyes in a portrait or photograph appear to follow the viewer as they move relative to the image. The effect is named after Leonardo da Vinci's famous Mona Lisa painting, whose subject's gaze famously seems to track observers as they walk past. This illusion occurs because flat, two-dimensional representations of faces lack the binocular disparity and motion parallax cues that normally signal changes in relative position. When viewing a photograph or painting, the depicted eyes maintain the same orientation relative to the viewer regardless of viewing angle, creating the impression that the gaze direction is actively tracking the observer. The phenomenon is not actually specific to the Mona Lisa but occurs with any frontal or near-frontal portrait where the subject appears to make eye contact with the camera or viewer. Research has shown that the illusion is particularly strong when the depicted gaze direction is within approximately 5 degrees of direct eye contact. The Mona Lisa Illusion demonstrates the visual system's sophisticated processing of social cues and gaze direction, which are crucial for human interaction. The effect involves both low-level visual processing of eye orientation and high-level interpretations of social attention and intentionality, making it a rich example of how perception integrates physical stimulus properties with social cognitive expectations.
