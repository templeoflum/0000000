## Abstract

Chronostasis is a temporal illusion in which the first visual stimulus fixated immediately following a saccadic eye movement appears to persist for a longer duration than it objectively does. The phenomenon is most commonly experienced when glancing at an analog clock with a second hand; the first second after the eye movement seems to last noticeably longer than subsequent seconds, creating the impression that the clock has momentarily stopped. This illusion demonstrates that subjective time perception is not veridical but instead reflects active neural construction involving motor efference copies, predictive coding, and postdictive temporal binding. During saccades, vision is suppressed to prevent smearing of the visual field, but the brain appears to compensate by backdating the onset of the post-saccadic stimulus to the time the eye movement was initiated. This temporal expansion can extend the perceived duration of the first fixated stimulus by up to 500 milliseconds. Chronostasis reveals fundamental principles about how the visual system maintains temporal continuity across discontinuous sampling of the environment during eye movements, and it has implications for understanding consciousness, temporal binding, and the relationship between perception and action.
