## Abstract

The Fraser-Wilcox Illusion, discovered by James Fraser and J.E.H. Wilcox in 1979, produces a compelling perception of rotary motion in static images composed of repeated asymmetric luminance profiles arranged in concentric circular patterns. The illusion typically consists of rings containing sequences of luminance elements that transition from light to dark through intermediate gray values in a spatially asymmetric manner. When observers view these patterns, they perceive continuous rotation or spinning motion, with the direction of perceived motion corresponding to the direction of luminance asymmetry within each ring. The effect can be quite strong, producing vivid impressions of continuous wheel-like rotation despite the complete absence of physical movement. Small eye movements and fixation shifts appear to contribute to the strength of the illusory motion, though the percept can persist even during attempted steady fixation. The Fraser-Wilcox Illusion is considered a foundational example of peripheral drift illusions and has inspired numerous related designs including the Rotating Snakes illusion. The phenomenon reveals how motion-sensitive neurons can be activated by specific spatial configurations of luminance gradients, particularly when these patterns interact with retinal image motion from microsaccades and other involuntary eye movements. Understanding this illusion provides insights into the spatiotemporal filtering properties of motion detection mechanisms in early visual processing.
