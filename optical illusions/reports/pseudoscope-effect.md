## Abstract

The Pseudoscope Effect refers to the perceptual consequences of viewing the world through a pseudoscope, an optical device invented by Charles Wheatstone in 1852 that reverses binocular disparity by swapping the images presented to the left and right eyes. This reversal inverts depth relationships, causing near objects to appear far and far objects to appear near, effectively turning the three-dimensional world inside out. When viewing familiar objects through a pseudoscope, observers experience profound depth cue conflicts, as the reversed stereoscopic information contradicts monocular cues such as perspective, occlusion, shading, and prior knowledge about object structure. The visual system often resolves these conflicts in favor of familiar interpretations, leading to bizarre perceptual experiences where faces appear hollow or convex surfaces appear concave. The effect demonstrates the hierarchical nature of depth processing and the powerful influence of top-down expectations on perceptual organization. Strong prior knowledge about object shape can override contradictory binocular disparity signals, revealing that depth perception involves active inference rather than passive registration of retinal signals. The pseudoscope has been employed in vision science research to dissociate different depth cues and investigate how the brain integrates conflicting spatial information, providing crucial insights into the computational principles underlying three-dimensional vision.
