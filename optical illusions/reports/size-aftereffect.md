## Abstract

The size aftereffect is an adaptation phenomenon in which prolonged viewing of objects of a particular size causes subsequently viewed objects to appear shifted in size in the opposite direction. After adapting to large objects or stimuli, test objects of moderate size appear smaller than baseline, while adaptation to small objects makes test stimuli appear larger. The effect operates across various stimulus types including simple geometric shapes, spatial frequency gratings, and complex objects. The magnitude of the aftereffect depends on the ratio between adaptation and test sizes, following Weber's law for size perception, and is maximal when adaptation and test stimuli are presented at the same retinal location, indicating retinotopic specificity. However, some degree of transfer across spatial position suggests involvement of higher-level size representations. The size aftereffect can be explained by adaptation of spatial frequency channels in early visual cortex, where different populations of neurons are selectively responsive to different spatial scales, or by adaptation in higher-level object representations that encode object size independently of low-level features. The phenomenon demonstrates the adaptive and relative nature of size perception, where perceived size is continuously calibrated based on recent visual experience rather than being an absolute property of the visual input.
