## Abstract

The Baldwin Illusion is a geometric size illusion in which identical lines appear to differ in length depending on the size of surrounding contextual elements. First described by James Mark Baldwin in 1895, the illusion typically presents two parallel horizontal lines of equal length, each positioned between pairs of squares or other geometric shapes. The line flanked by larger squares appears shorter than the line flanked by smaller squares, despite their objective equality. This perceptual distortion demonstrates how the visual system integrates size information from context when evaluating the magnitude of visual features. The Baldwin Illusion shares underlying mechanisms with other context-dependent size illusions, particularly the Ebbinghaus illusion, and provides evidence for assimilation effects in size perception. Theoretical accounts emphasize misapplied size constancy scaling, whereby the visual system inappropriately applies depth or size relationships from surrounding elements to the target lines. The illusion has been studied extensively in psychophysical experiments that manipulate the ratio of contextual element size to target line length, revealing systematic patterns in the magnitude of the perceptual effect. Its robust nature across viewing conditions makes it a valuable tool for investigating the mechanisms of contextual size perception.
