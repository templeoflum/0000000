## Abstract

The Rotating Snakes Illusion, created by Akiyoshi Kitaoka in 2003, is a striking illusory motion phenomenon in which static circular patterns composed of specific luminance sequences produce strong percepts of continuous rotation. The stimulus consists of concentric rings containing repeating sequences of colored or grayscale elements arranged in a particular order, typically progressing from dark to light. When viewed in peripheral vision or during small eye movements such as microsaccades, the circular patterns appear to rotate spontaneously and continuously, despite being completely static. The illusion is most pronounced when fixation shifts or during brief saccades, and the perceived rotation direction corresponds systematically to the luminance gradient direction in the pattern. The Rotating Snakes Illusion has become one of the most widely recognized examples of illusory motion from static images. Its mechanisms likely involve asymmetric temporal processing of luminance changes by motion-sensitive neurons, possibly related to differential latencies in processing dark versus light stimuli, combined with neural adaptation and the specific spatiotemporal characteristics of eye movements and attentional shifts.
