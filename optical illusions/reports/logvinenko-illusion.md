## Abstract

The Logvinenko Illusion demonstrates that perceived lightness depends critically on the spatial structure and configuration of luminance gradients in the visual field, rather than solely on absolute luminance values or simple contrast ratios. In this illusion, target patches of identical luminance embedded in different gradient configurations appear dramatically different in lightness. The effect reveals that the visual system analyzes the spatial derivative structure of the luminance distribution to infer surface reflectance properties and illumination patterns. Logvinenko's work challenged traditional theories based on luminance ratios or edge detection alone, showing that the global spatial arrangement of luminance transitions plays a fundamental role in lightness computation. The illusion provides evidence that the visual system performs sophisticated spatial filtering operations to decompose the retinal image into components attributed to reflectance versus illumination. The magnitude of the perceived lightness difference varies systematically with the steepness, orientation, and spatial frequency characteristics of the surrounding gradients. This phenomenon has influenced computational models of lightness perception, particularly those incorporating multi-scale spatial filtering and intrinsic image decomposition. The Logvinenko Illusion underscores that lightness is not a local property computed independently at each retinal location, but emerges from analysis of extended spatial patterns.
