## Abstract

Illusion knitting, also known as shadow knitting, is a textile technique that produces knitted fabric containing hidden images or patterns that become visible only when the fabric is viewed from an oblique angle, appearing as uniform texture when viewed perpendicularly. The technique relies on alternating rows of different stitch types that create varying degrees of relief or texture in the fabric surface. When viewed straight-on, these textural variations are subtle and blend together, but when viewed at an acute angle, the raised stitches cast shadows or catch light differentially, revealing the concealed design. This phenomenon demonstrates principles of spatial filtering and orientation-selective processing in the visual system, as the viewing angle effectively changes the spatial frequency content and orientation distribution of the visual stimulus reaching the eye. Illusion knitting exploits the visual system's sensitivity to luminance gradients created by surface relief under directional lighting, as well as texture segmentation processes that become more pronounced under oblique viewing conditions. The technique represents a sophisticated manipulation of three-dimensional surface properties to control two-dimensional appearance as a function of viewpoint, illustrating how the same physical stimulus can produce dramatically different percepts depending on viewing geometry. Contemporary fiber artists have expanded the technique to create reversible garments, interactive art pieces, and demonstrations of viewpoint-dependent perception in textile form.
