## Abstract

The Tolansky illusion, described by physicist Samuel Tolansky in 1964, is a geometric illusion involving systematic misperception of line alignment, orientation, or length in specific spatial configurations. Tolansky, known primarily for his work in optical physics and interferometry, contributed several observations on visual perception anomalies, particularly those involving periodic patterns, alignment judgments, and interactions between line elements. While less widely known than canonical illusions such as the Müller-Lyer or Poggendorff, the Tolansky illusion exemplifies how subtle variations in spatial arrangement can induce robust perceptual distortions. The specific parameters and stimulus configuration vary across reports, but common elements include arrays of lines or segments where context influences judgments of collinearity, parallelism, or relative length. Theoretical accounts invoke mechanisms of contour integration, in which the visual system's algorithms for linking discontinuous edges or extrapolating trajectories are biased by surrounding elements, and spatial filtering models that predict distortions based on interactions between oriented filters at multiple scales. The illusion contributes to the broader family of geometric illusions that reveal systematic biases in spatial vision and has been studied in the context of understanding how configural context modulates local metric judgments. Further empirical investigation is needed to fully characterize its parameters and distinguish it from related phenomena.
