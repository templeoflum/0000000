## Abstract

The Shepard tone is an auditory illusion of a tone that appears to ascend or descend continuously in pitch without ever getting higher or lower, creating the impression of an infinite staircase in pitch space. Created by Roger Shepard in 1964, the illusion is produced by superimposing sine wave components separated by octaves, with each component's amplitude controlled by a bell-shaped spectral envelope that remains fixed as the frequencies glide upward or downward. As higher frequency components fade out at the top of the envelope, lower components simultaneously fade in at the bottom, creating the perceptual experience of continuous directional change despite the cyclic physical structure. The illusion exploits the fact that tones separated by octaves are perceived as similar due to the circular nature of pitch chroma, which is independent of pitch height. The Shepard tone demonstrates that pitch perception involves both a linear dimension (height) and a circular dimension (chroma), with the illusion arising from the dissociation between these two aspects. Variants include the Shepard scale and the continuously gliding Shepard-Risset glissando. The phenomenon has been used extensively in psychoacoustic research, film sound design, and music composition to create tension and ambiguity, and provides insights into the cognitive representation of auditory dimensions.
