## Abstract

Cloud pareidolia refers to the perceptual phenomenon in which observers perceive recognizable shapes, objects, or figures in the ambiguous and continuously changing patterns of clouds. Most commonly, people report seeing faces, animals, or familiar objects in cloud formations, despite the stimulus consisting only of water vapor and ice crystals shaped by atmospheric processes. This form of pareidolia represents a particularly dynamic instance of pattern recognition, as clouds constantly shift and transform, allowing for multiple interpretations and encouraging prolonged observation. The phenomenon is nearly universal across cultures and age groups, suggesting fundamental aspects of human visual cognition. Cloud pareidolia has been documented throughout human history in art, literature, and folklore, with various cultures attributing spiritual or prophetic significance to perceived cloud shapes. From a cognitive neuroscience perspective, cloud pareidolia exemplifies the brain's tendency to impose familiar templates onto ambiguous visual information, particularly when stimuli contain elements resembling eyes, faces, or biologically relevant configurations. The low spatial frequency information and edge fragments present in cloud boundaries appear sufficient to trigger object recognition systems, even in the absence of confirmatory detail. Research on cloud pareidolia provides insights into the balance between signal detection sensitivity and specificity in human perception.
