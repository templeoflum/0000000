## Abstract

The atmospheric corona is a colored ring or series of concentric colored rings visible around the sun or moon when viewed through thin clouds composed of water droplets or ice crystals of relatively uniform size. This optical phenomenon results from diffraction of light as it passes around the individual cloud particles, with the diffracted light waves interfering constructively and destructively at different angles to produce the characteristic colored rings. The innermost region, called the aureole, appears as a bright disc of whitish or bluish light surrounding the light source, while additional colored rings may extend outward with red on the outer edges and blue toward the inner edges of each ring. The angular size and spacing of the corona rings are inversely related to the size of the cloud droplets: smaller droplets produce larger coronas, while larger droplets create smaller, more tightly spaced rings. The visibility and vividness of the corona depend critically on the uniformity of droplet sizes within the cloud; clouds with droplets of mixed sizes produce washed-out or indistinct coronas, while clouds with highly uniform droplet sizes generate brilliantly colored, well-defined ring systems. Coronas are most commonly observed around the moon because solar coronas require the sun to be obscured or viewed indirectly to prevent eye damage.
