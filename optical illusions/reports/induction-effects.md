## Abstract

Induction effects constitute a broad class of contextual phenomena in which the perceptual appearance of a target stimulus is systematically altered by the properties of surrounding or adjacent stimuli. These effects span multiple perceptual dimensions including brightness, color, size, orientation, and motion, and demonstrate that perception is fundamentally relational rather than absolute. Classical examples include simultaneous brightness contrast, where a gray patch appears lighter on a dark background than on a light background, and color contrast, where complementary surrounds induce opponent hues in neutral targets. Induction can occur through spatial adjacency, as in simultaneous contrast, or through temporal succession, as in successive contrast and aftereffects. The magnitude of induction typically increases with spatial proximity and similarity between inducing and test stimuli. Induction effects are thought to arise from multiple mechanisms operating at different levels of visual processing, including lateral inhibition at early stages, normalization processes at intermediate stages, and higher-level contextual interpretation. These phenomena reveal the visual system's strategy of encoding relative rather than absolute properties, which provides stability across varying illumination and enhances the detection of edges and boundaries critical for segmentation and object recognition.
