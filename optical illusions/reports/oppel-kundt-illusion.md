## Abstract

The Oppel-Kundt Illusion, also known as the Filled Space Illusion, was first described by Johann Joseph Oppel in 1855 and later investigated systematically by August Kundt. The illusion demonstrates that a linear spatial extent filled with regularly spaced elements such as dots, lines, or other markers appears substantially longer than an equal empty extent, with magnitude typically ranging from 5-15% overestimation depending on the number and spacing of filling elements. The effect is most pronounced with moderate filling densities and diminishes with very sparse or very dense element arrangements. This robust phenomenon has been studied extensively across different spatial scales, orientations, and modalities. Theoretical explanations have invoked attentional mechanisms, where filled intervals attract more processing resources, and spatial integration accounts, where the visual system integrates or counts individual elements, leading to expanded perceived extent. Contemporary models emphasize spatial filtering operations in early visual cortex, where filled intervals generate more neural activity across orientation-selective and spatial frequency channels than empty intervals, potentially biasing length estimates. The illusion has practical significance for understanding spatial layout perception and has been investigated in the context of architectural and design applications where perceived space matters. The Oppel-Kundt Illusion reveals fundamental principles about how texture and element density influence metric spatial judgments.
