## Abstract

The Rhombille Tiling Illusion arises from periodic arrangements of rhombi (diamond-shaped parallelograms) that the visual system spontaneously interprets as three-dimensional cube structures, despite the pattern being objectively flat. This geometric tiling can be perceived in at least three distinct orientations, each presenting the cubes as stacked in a different direction, with the pattern appearing to shift between these interpretations through multistable perception. The illusion demonstrates how the brain preferentially interprets certain two-dimensional geometric configurations as three-dimensional objects, particularly when the stimulus contains angles and adjacency relationships consistent with familiar polyhedra viewed in parallel or isometric projection. The rhombille pattern exploits the visual system's bias toward interpreting acute and obtuse angles as perspective views of right angles, a tendency related to the oblique effect and the prevalence of rectangular structures in human environments. When properly shaded or colored, the depth interpretation becomes even more compelling, with specific rhombi appearing as visible cube faces and others forming partially occluded surfaces. The phenomenon is closely related to other multistable depth percepts including the Necker cube and impossible figures, revealing fundamental principles about how geometric regularity, grouping principles, and depth-from-contour processes interact to construct three-dimensional interpretations from two-dimensional patterns.
