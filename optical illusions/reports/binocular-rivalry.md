## Abstract

Binocular rivalry is a fundamental visual phenomenon that occurs when two incompatible images are presented simultaneously, one to each eye. Rather than perceiving a stable blend or superposition of the two images, observers experience spontaneous alternations in which one image dominates conscious perception while the other is suppressed, with dominance switching back and forth every few seconds. First systematically described by Charles Wheatstone in 1838 using his newly invented stereoscope, binocular rivalry has become one of the most extensively studied paradigms in visual neuroscience for investigating the neural correlates of conscious perception. The phenomenon demonstrates that perceptual awareness is not a simple reflection of sensory input but rather emerges from competitive interactions between neural representations. The alternations are largely involuntary, though attention and other cognitive factors can bias dominance durations. Rivalry occurs at multiple levels of the visual hierarchy, from eye-based competition in early visual areas to stimulus-based competition involving higher-level representations. Individual differences in rivalry dynamics are stable over time and have been linked to genetic factors and clinical conditions. The study of binocular rivalry has provided crucial insights into neural mechanisms of perceptual selection, consciousness, and the relationship between neural activity and subjective experience.
