## Abstract

The Bourdon Illusion, discovered by French psychologist Benjamin Bourdon in 1902, is a geometric distortion illusion in which objectively straight edges of a figure appear curved or bent. The illusion typically occurs when a rectilinear figure, such as a rectangle or square, is viewed in the context of other geometric patterns or when specific internal features create local orientation conflicts. Observers consistently perceive deviations from straightness along edges that are physically linear, demonstrating systematic misperception of geometric properties. The phenomenon illustrates how the visual system's contour integration mechanisms can produce distortions when local orientation signals conflict with global shape information. Theoretical explanations emphasize the role of orientation-selective neurons in early visual cortex, which respond to local edge segments and may produce biased estimates of overall contour shape when integration processes encounter specific spatial configurations. The Bourdon Illusion belongs to a broader class of geometric distortion phenomena that reveal constraints and computational strategies in shape perception. Experimental investigations have examined how factors such as viewing distance, stimulus size, and internal pattern complexity modulate the strength of the illusory curvature, providing insights into the spatial scale at which orientation information is pooled during contour processing.
