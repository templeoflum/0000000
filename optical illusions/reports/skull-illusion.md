## Abstract

The Skull Illusion refers to a class of ambiguous images that can be perceived either as a human skull or as an alternative scene, typically depicting two human figures, a landscape, or other objects arranged such that their contours and shading create the appearance of a skull when viewed as a unified whole. This type of double image demonstrates sophisticated perceptual organization where the same visual elements participate in two radically different interpretations depending on the scale and grouping strategy applied by the observer. When perceived as a skull, specific features such as eye sockets, nasal cavity, and teeth emerge from the global configuration, while the alternative interpretation requires parsing the image into separate figural elements at a finer spatial scale. The bistability arises from competition between local and global organizational processes, with attention alternating between detailed scrutiny of individual elements and holistic perception of the overall pattern. Such skull illusions have historical roots in vanitas art and memento mori traditions, where hidden skull imagery carried symbolic meaning. From a perceptual science perspective, these stimuli reveal how the visual system can extract multiple levels of structure from the same sensory input and how semantic expectations influence organizational preferences. Research using skull and similar hierarchical ambiguous figures has illuminated interactions between spatial frequency processing, figure-ground segregation, and top-down semantic influences in perceptual organization.
