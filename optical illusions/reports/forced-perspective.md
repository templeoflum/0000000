## Abstract

Forced perspective is a photographic and theatrical technique that exploits depth cue conflicts to create illusions of altered object size, distance, or spatial relationships. By carefully positioning objects at different distances from the camera or observer while controlling the viewing geometry, practitioners can make objects appear much larger or smaller than their actual physical dimensions. The technique works by manipulating the relationship between retinal image size and perceived distance, taking advantage of size constancy mechanisms that normally compensate for distance when judging object size. Common applications include making people appear giant or miniature, creating impossible spatial configurations, or exaggerating architectural features. When depth cues are minimized through monocular viewing, limited visual context, or shallow depth of field, the visual system has insufficient information to accurately determine object distances, allowing retinal size to dominate size perception. The illusion is particularly effective in two-dimensional media such as photographs and films, where binocular disparity cues are absent. Forced perspective has been extensively employed in cinema special effects, tourist photography, architectural design, and theme park attractions. The phenomenon demonstrates the visual system's reliance on consistent depth-size relationships and reveals how violations of these relationships can produce dramatic perceptual effects. Understanding forced perspective requires knowledge of perspective geometry, depth cue integration, and the computational principles underlying size constancy.
