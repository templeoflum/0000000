## Abstract

The Pulsating Illusion refers to a class of visual phenomena in which static geometric patterns appear to rhythmically expand, contract, or pulsate despite containing no physical motion or luminance modulation. These illusions typically involve radial or concentric configurations with high-contrast edges, spatial frequency gradients, or alternating light-dark regions that create dynamic percepts when viewed steadily or during small eye movements. The pulsating quality often manifests as a breathing or throbbing sensation, with regions appearing to grow and shrink in synchrony or in counter-phase. The illusion is particularly pronounced in peripheral vision and can be enhanced by varying the luminance, color contrast, or spatial scale of the pattern. Mechanistic accounts emphasize the role of motion-energy detectors that respond to spatial asymmetries in luminance profiles, combined with neural adaptation effects that create temporal fluctuations in response strength. Fixational eye movements such as microsaccades and drift may contribute by generating differential motion signals across oriented edges, while attention and expectation can modulate the perceived strength and coherence of the pulsation. The Pulsating Illusion demonstrates that static spatial structure can drive dynamic perceptual experiences through interactions between form-processing and motion-processing pathways, revealing fundamental principles of spatiotemporal integration in visual cortex.
