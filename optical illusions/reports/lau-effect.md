## Abstract

The Lau Effect is a visual phenomenon discovered by Ernst Lau in 1948, characterized by the appearance of visible fringes or patterns when two incoherent gratings are superimposed. Unlike traditional moiré patterns that arise from geometric interference of periodic structures, the Lau Effect emerges from the interaction of spatially incoherent illumination with periodic structures, producing intensity modulations that the visual system interprets as distinct patterns. The effect demonstrates the visual system's sensitivity to second-order spatial correlations and statistical regularities in the visual field, even when first-order luminance information appears uniform or random. The Lau Effect has theoretical significance for understanding spatial filtering mechanisms in early visual processing, particularly how the visual system extracts structure from seemingly noisy or incoherent stimuli. It shares conceptual similarities with moiré phenomena but involves fundamentally different physical and perceptual mechanisms related to spatial coherence and autocorrelation functions. The effect has applications in optics, image processing, and understanding how biological vision systems detect regularities in complex visual environments. Research on the Lau Effect bridges physical optics, computational vision, and perceptual psychology, offering insights into the statistical processing capabilities of human visual perception.
