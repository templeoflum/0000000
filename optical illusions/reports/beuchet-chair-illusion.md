## Abstract

The Beuchet Chair Illusion is a three-dimensional installation in which a chair is divided into separate components positioned at different distances from the viewer, yet appears to form a complete, normal-sized chair when viewed from a specific vantage point. Developed by Jean Beuchet, this demonstration creates dramatic misperceptions of human body size when individuals interact with the apparently unified chair structure. The illusion exploits depth cue conflicts and size constancy mechanisms by carefully positioning the chair's back, seat, and legs at varying depths while maintaining the correct angular projections from the designated viewing position. When a person sits in the chair, their body parts occupy different depth planes, causing the visual system to apply inconsistent size scaling across the figure. This results in the person appearing drastically distorted, with body parts seeming impossibly large or small relative to one another. The effect is particularly striking because the chair itself appears perceptually coherent and normal-sized from the critical viewpoint, making the size distortions of the human figure unexpected and counterintuitive. The Beuchet Chair demonstrates how the visual system integrates depth information to maintain size constancy for familiar objects, and how violations of consistent depth relationships can produce dramatic perceptual consequences for perceived object dimensions.
