## Abstract

Expectation-driven illusions constitute a broad class of perceptual phenomena where prior expectations, beliefs, or contextual knowledge systematically bias perception away from the veridical properties of sensory input. These illusions demonstrate that perception is not a passive registration of sensory data but an active inference process that combines bottom-up sensory evidence with top-down predictions derived from past experience and current context. Examples span all sensory modalities and include phenomena such as perceptual set effects, where prior exposure to ambiguous stimuli influences subsequent interpretation; the influence of semantic context on speech perception; and visual illusions where knowledge about typical object properties overrides contrary sensory evidence. The magnitude of expectation-driven effects typically increases when sensory input is ambiguous, degraded, or impoverished, consistent with Bayesian accounts in which prior probabilities have greater influence when sensory likelihood is weak. Individual differences in susceptibility to these illusions may reflect differences in the relative weighting of sensory versus prior information. Contemporary predictive coding frameworks treat expectation-driven biases as a fundamental feature of neural computation, with perception representing the brain's best hypothesis about the causes of sensory input.
