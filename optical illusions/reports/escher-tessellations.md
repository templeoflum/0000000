## Abstract

Escher tessellations are interlocking tile patterns developed by Dutch artist M.C. Escher in which recognizable figures such as birds, fish, reptiles, or other forms completely cover a plane without gaps or overlaps, often with adjacent tiles representing different or complementary figures. These mathematical artworks exploit principles of symmetry, periodic tiling, and figure-ground organization to create visually compelling demonstrations of how the perceptual system segments complex scenes into meaningful objects. The tessellations frequently incorporate metamorphosis sequences where one form gradually transforms into another across the plane, challenging the visual system's object recognition processes and revealing the flexibility of contour assignment in figure-ground segregation. Escher's work demonstrates that shared contours between adjacent regions can simultaneously serve as the boundary of multiple distinct perceptual objects, a principle that violates typical assumptions about edge ownership in visual processing. The artistic achievement lies in creating tiles that are both mathematically precise in their geometric properties and perceptually meaningful as recognizable entities. These tessellations have become iconic demonstrations of the intersection between mathematics, art, and perceptual psychology, illustrating fundamental principles of spatial organization, symmetry perception, and the cognitive processes underlying object recognition from ambiguous or overlapping visual information.
