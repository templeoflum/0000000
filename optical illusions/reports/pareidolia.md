## Abstract

Pareidolia is the perceptual tendency to perceive meaningful patterns, particularly faces and familiar objects, in random, ambiguous, or unstructured stimuli. This phenomenon represents a fundamental aspect of human perception in which the visual system actively constructs interpretations of ambiguous sensory input by matching it against stored representations and expectations. Pareidolia occurs across multiple sensory modalities, though it is most commonly studied and experienced in vision, where people perceive faces in clouds, religious figures in toast, animals in rock formations, and countless other meaningful patterns in noise. The tendency appears to be universal across human cultures and developmental stages, suggesting it reflects core properties of perceptual organization rather than learned cultural practices. From an evolutionary perspective, pareidolia may represent an adaptive bias: the cost of missing a meaningful pattern such as a predator or social partner is typically much higher than the cost of occasionally perceiving patterns that are not actually present. Modern theoretical accounts frame pareidolia within Bayesian and predictive coding frameworks, where strong prior expectations about common patterns like faces can override weak or ambiguous bottom-up sensory evidence. Neural imaging studies have demonstrated that pareidolic perceptions activate the same cortical regions involved in perceiving actual objects and faces, indicating that these illusory percepts engage high-level visual processing systems. Pareidolia has implications for understanding perception, pattern recognition, false pattern detection, and the balance between sensitivity and specificity in cognitive systems.
