## Abstract

Persistence of vision refers to the phenomenon in which visual percepts continue briefly after the physical stimulus has been removed, creating a temporal continuity that enables the perception of smooth motion from rapidly presented discrete images. This property of the visual system has been historically invoked to explain the apparent motion seen in cinema, television, animation, and early optical devices such as the phenakistoscope and zoetrope. The persistence duration is typically estimated at approximately 50-100 milliseconds, though this varies with stimulus intensity, duration, and retinal location. While early accounts attributed persistence of vision primarily to retinal photoreceptor afterimages, contemporary understanding recognizes multiple neural mechanisms operating at different levels of the visual hierarchy, including photoreceptor transduction kinetics, retinal ganglion cell temporal integration, and cortical temporal binding processes. The concept has been somewhat controversial in vision science, with debates about whether persistence alone can account for apparent motion perception or whether higher-level motion processing mechanisms are primarily responsible. Despite these theoretical debates, persistence of vision remains a foundational concept for understanding temporal aspects of visual perception and the historical development of motion picture technology.
