## Abstract

Illusory contours, also known as subjective contours, are perceived edges and boundaries that appear in regions of an image where no physical luminance gradient or other local image feature directly signals the presence of an edge. These contours arise from the spatial arrangement of inducing elements such as line terminators, angle vertices, or phase-shifted gratings, which suggest the presence of an occluding surface even though no surface is physically present in the stimulus. Observers report clear perception of defined edges with specific orientation and position, and these illusory boundaries can support figure-ground segregation, depth ordering, and other perceptual judgments in much the same way as physically specified contours. Illusory contours demonstrate that edge detection in the visual system is not simply a matter of identifying local luminance discontinuities but involves integrating information across space and making inferences about scene structure based on the configuration of visible features. The phenomenon has been extensively studied using both psychophysical and neurophysiological methods, revealing that neurons in early and mid-level visual cortex respond to illusory contours in a manner similar to their responses to real luminance-defined edges. Illusory contours have become a paradigmatic case for understanding how the visual system constructs explicit representations of scene structure through active interpolation and completion processes.
