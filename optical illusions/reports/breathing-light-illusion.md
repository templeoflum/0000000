## Abstract

The Breathing Light Illusion is a dynamic visual phenomenon in which a static luminance pattern appears to pulsate or breathe rhythmically, creating the impression of periodic expansions and contractions in brightness or intensity. Despite the objective stability of the stimulus, observers perceive slow, wave-like modulations that give the appearance of the pattern inhaling and exhaling. This illusory motion typically manifests as periodic changes in perceived brightness, size, or both, with the rhythm of the perceived breathing varying across different stimulus configurations and viewing conditions. The effect is particularly pronounced in patterns containing specific spatial frequency content and luminance gradients, and can be enhanced by peripheral viewing or subtle eye movements. The illusion demonstrates the visual system's sensitivity to temporal variations even in the absence of physical change, likely arising from interactions between spatial filtering mechanisms, neural adaptation processes, and low-level motion detection systems. Small involuntary eye movements, such as microsaccades and drift, may contribute to the effect by causing temporal modulations in the retinal image that are interpreted as genuine stimulus changes. The breathing light illusion provides insight into the dynamic nature of visual processing and the challenges the visual system faces in distinguishing between observer-generated and stimulus-generated changes.
