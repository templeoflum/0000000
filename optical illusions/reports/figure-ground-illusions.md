## Abstract

Figure-Ground Illusions constitute a general class of visual stimuli in which the assignment of image regions as figure (foreground object) versus ground (background) is ambiguous, resulting in multiple possible perceptual organizations of the same visual input. These illusions demonstrate that figure-ground segregation is not a trivial byproduct of the stimulus but rather an active perceptual achievement involving complex neural computations. The most famous example is Rubin's vase-face illusion, in which a single contour can be assigned either to a central vase or to two facing profiles, but never to both simultaneously. Figure-ground illusions reveal several fundamental principles of perceptual organization identified by Gestalt psychologists, including surroundedness, symmetry, convexity, size, and orientation. Regions that are surrounded, symmetric, convex, smaller, or vertically oriented tend to be perceived as figure rather than ground. Figure regions are perceived as having definite shape and appearing closer to the observer, while ground regions appear shapeless and to continue behind the figure. The neural mechanisms underlying figure-ground segregation involve complex interactions across multiple stages of visual processing, from early border ownership assignment in V1 and V2 to higher-level object recognition processes. These illusions have profound implications for understanding how the brain parses complex visual scenes into meaningful objects and their spatial relationships.
