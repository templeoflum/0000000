## Abstract

The Duck-Rabbit Illusion is one of the most iconic and widely recognized examples of bistable perception, in which a single ambiguous line drawing can be perceived as either a duck facing left or a rabbit facing right. While the image appeared in German magazines in the late 19th century, it was popularized by American psychologist Joseph Jastrow in 1899 as a demonstration of the active role of perception in organizing sensory information. The same visual features serve dual purposes: what appears as the duck's bill becomes the rabbit's ears, and vice versa. The illusion demonstrates that a single retinal image can support multiple mutually exclusive perceptual interpretations, with observers experiencing spontaneous alternations between the two percepts. The ease and speed with which individuals can switch between interpretations has been studied in relation to cognitive flexibility, creativity, and age-related changes in perceptual processing. Children tend to find it more difficult to switch between interpretations than adults, suggesting developmental changes in cognitive control over perception. The Duck-Rabbit has been extensively used in philosophy of mind to discuss the relationship between seeing and seeing-as, and remains a powerful demonstration that perception involves interpretation rather than passive reception of sensory data.
