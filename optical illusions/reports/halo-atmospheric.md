## Abstract

Atmospheric halos are optical phenomena appearing as rings, arcs, or bright spots around the sun or moon, caused primarily by refraction and reflection of light through hexagonal ice crystals suspended in the upper atmosphere, typically in cirrus or cirrostratus clouds. The most common halo is the 22-degree halo, appearing as a ring with an angular radius of approximately 22 degrees from the sun or moon, resulting from light refraction through ice crystals with a prism angle of 60 degrees. Less common is the 46-degree halo, produced by light passing through crystals oriented differently. The phenomenon has been documented for millennia, with ancient observers often interpreting halos as meteorological omens or divine signs. The appearance of halos depends on the precise geometry of ice crystal shapes, orientations, and sizes, as well as the observer's position relative to the light source. While the basic 22-degree halo can appear relatively frequently when appropriate cirrus clouds are present, more complex halo phenomena including circumzenithal arcs, parhelic circles, and tangent arcs require specific crystal orientations and are correspondingly rarer. The study of atmospheric halos has contributed significantly to understanding atmospheric optics, cloud microphysics, and the optical properties of ice crystals at various temperatures and humidity conditions.
