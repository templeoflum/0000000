## Abstract

The Müller-Lyer Illusion, first reported by German psychiatrist Franz Carl Müller-Lyer in 1889, stands as perhaps the most extensively studied geometric illusion in perceptual science. The classic configuration presents two equal-length horizontal line segments, one terminated by outward-pointing arrow fins (wings-out) and the other by inward-pointing fins (wings-in). Observers consistently perceive the wings-out shaft as approximately 15-25% longer than the wings-in shaft, despite their objective equality. This robust illusion persists across repeated exposures and conscious awareness of the equivalence, demonstrating mandatory perceptual processing. Theoretical explanations have proliferated since its discovery, with the misapplied size constancy theory proposing that the fin configurations resemble perspective cues for depth, triggering automatic scaling mechanisms that evolved for three-dimensional scene interpretation. Alternative accounts invoke spatial filtering by oriented receptive fields, assimilation and contrast effects, and Bayesian inference frameworks where the visual system integrates prior expectations about typical three-dimensional configurations. The illusion shows cultural variation, with reduced magnitude in populations with less exposure to carpentered environments. The Müller-Lyer Illusion remains central to debates about the relationship between two-dimensional retinal geometry and three-dimensional perceptual interpretation, making it a touchstone for theories of spatial vision.
