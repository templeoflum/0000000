## Abstract

Gilchrist's Illusion is a lightness phenomenon that provides strong empirical support for anchoring theory, a framework developed by Alan Gilchrist to explain how the visual system computes surface lightness. The illusion demonstrates that physically identical surfaces can appear dramatically different in lightness depending on which spatial framework or grouping they are perceived to belong to. In typical demonstrations, a gray target surface is presented in a configuration where figural organization cues suggest that it belongs to one of two different frameworks, such as separate illumination fields or depth planes. When the target is perceived as belonging to a brightly illuminated framework, it appears darker, consistent with the visual system scaling its lightness relative to the highest luminance in that framework. When perceived as belonging to a dimly illuminated framework, the same target appears lighter. This effect reveals that lightness computation is not purely local but involves global or semi-global processes that identify perceptual frameworks and anchor lightness judgments within them. According to anchoring theory, the visual system assigns the highest luminance in a framework the lightness value of white, and scales other surfaces relative to this anchor. Gilchrist's demonstrations have been influential in establishing that lightness perception depends critically on perceptual grouping and scene segmentation, not merely on local luminance ratios.
