## Abstract

The Wertheimer-Benary Illusion is a lightness illusion demonstrating that perceived brightness is influenced by Gestalt grouping principles and figure-ground organization rather than simply by local luminance ratios. The classic configuration involves a cross or similar geometric figure with identical gray triangular targets positioned in different locations: one on the main body of the figure and one in a notch or adjacent region. Despite identical luminance and similar surrounding regions, the targets appear different in lightness. The triangle perceived as part of the main figure tends to appear lighter or darker in a direction consistent with belongingness to that figure, while the triangle in the notch shows different lightness shifts. Described by Gestalt psychologists Max Wertheimer and Wilhelm Benary in 1924, this illusion challenged early theories based purely on local contrast or area ratios. The phenomenon demonstrates that the visual system interprets lightness in terms of perceptual organization: surfaces grouped together tend to influence each other more strongly than spatially adjacent but perceptually segregated regions. The Wertheimer-Benary Illusion reveals that edge assignment, junction interpretation, and figural belongingness play crucial roles in lightness computation. The effect has been explained by anchoring theories, edge-integration models, and frameworks emphasizing the role of junctions and layer decomposition in establishing the reference frame for lightness judgments.
