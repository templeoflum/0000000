## Abstract

The No Curved Lines Illusion represents a class of geometric patterns in which physically straight line segments arranged in specific configurations produce a compelling perception of curvature. Despite being composed entirely of horizontal, vertical, or diagonal straight elements, the overall pattern appears to contain smooth curves, arcs, or wave-like distortions. This phenomenon is related to several well-documented illusion families, including the café wall illusion and the Fraser spiral illusion, all of which demonstrate how local orientation signals can be integrated into incorrect global shape percepts. The perceptual effect arises from the visual system's contour integration processes, which attempt to construct coherent continuous boundaries from discrete oriented elements. Theoretical accounts emphasize the role of orientation-selective neurons in primary visual cortex and their lateral interactions across the visual field. When locally oriented elements are arranged in particular spatial patterns, the pooling and integration mechanisms that normally serve to detect smooth contours in natural scenes can be systematically misled, producing illusory curvature where none exists. The illusion demonstrates fundamental principles of how early spatial filtering operations and mid-level contour grouping mechanisms can interact to produce emergent percepts that deviate from stimulus geometry, revealing the constructive nature of shape perception.
