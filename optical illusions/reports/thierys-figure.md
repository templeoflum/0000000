## Abstract

Thiéry's Figure, described by Belgian psychologist Armand Thiéry in 1896, is an ambiguous line drawing that alternates between two distinct three-dimensional interpretations of stacked blocks or books viewed from different orientations. The figure typically depicts a configuration that can be seen either as a set of volumes stacked horizontally with their spines facing the viewer, or as the same volumes arranged vertically when viewed from a different angle, with the depth relationships reversing between interpretations. This bistable depth illusion demonstrates how identical two-dimensional contours can support multiple three-dimensional structural interpretations in the absence of definitive depth cues. The spontaneous perceptual reversals occur because the line drawing provides insufficient information to uniquely specify the spatial configuration, allowing the visual system to oscillate between equally plausible organizational schemas. Thiéry's Figure belongs to a family of depth-ambiguous geometric figures that includes the Necker Cube and Schroeder Staircase, all of which reveal fundamental principles about how the visual system infers three-dimensional structure from two-dimensional images. Research using this and related stimuli has contributed to understanding the mechanisms of depth perception, the role of prior assumptions in resolving perceptual ambiguity, and the neural dynamics underlying bistable perception. The figure has been valuable for studying how attention and voluntary control influence perceptual alternations in depth-reversible stimuli.
