## Abstract

The Peripheral Drift Illusion produces a vivid perception of continuous rotation or translation in completely static patterns when viewed in peripheral vision. The effect is maximized by repeating asymmetric luminance profiles, typically consisting of sequential color or brightness steps arranged in sawtooth or staircase configurations along circular or spiral trajectories. When observers fixate near but not directly on these patterns, the configurations appear to rotate smoothly and persistently, with the direction of apparent motion determined by the polarity of the luminance gradient. The illusion vanishes under direct fixation and strengthens dramatically in the visual periphery, revealing fundamental differences in motion processing between foveal and peripheral retina. Current explanations emphasize temporal differences in the processing of light versus dark stimuli at the photoreceptor level, creating sequential activation of motion-sensitive neurons that integrate local signals into coherent directional motion percepts. The Peripheral Drift Illusion has gained widespread recognition through artistic installations and internet demonstrations, and provides insights into the neural mechanisms underlying peripheral motion sensitivity, the role of fixational eye movements in motion perception, and the relationship between spatial attention and illusory motion strength.
