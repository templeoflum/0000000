## Abstract

The Face on Mars refers to a mesa formation in the Cydonia region of Mars that appeared to resemble a humanoid face in images captured by NASA's Viking 1 orbiter on July 25, 1976. The original photograph, taken at a resolution of approximately 43 meters per pixel under specific illumination conditions, showed what appeared to be two eyes, a nose, and a mouth arranged in face-like configuration. This image became one of the most famous examples of pareidolia in the space age, generating substantial public interest and speculation about artificial structures on Mars. The Face on Mars illusion demonstrates how low-resolution imagery combined with familiar lighting conditions can trigger face detection mechanisms in human visual processing. When higher-resolution images were obtained by Mars Global Surveyor in 1998 and 2001, and later by Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, the face-like appearance was revealed to be an artifact of low resolution, viewing angle, and lighting conditions. The mesa is actually an eroded landform with no face-like features when viewed at higher resolution or from different angles. This case study illustrates the powerful influence of expectation and face-detection biases on perception, and has become a canonical example in discussions of pareidolia, critical thinking, and the interpretation of ambiguous scientific data.
