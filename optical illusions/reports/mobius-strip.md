## Abstract

The Möbius Strip represents a unique class of perceptual and cognitive illusions arising from topological properties that violate ordinary spatial intuitions. Named after mathematician August Ferdinand Möbius, this single-sided, non-orientable surface challenges fundamental assumptions about inside versus outside, front versus back, and continuous orientation in three-dimensional space. When observers trace a path along the surface of a Möbius strip, they return to their starting point having traversed what appears to be both sides of the surface without crossing an edge, creating a profound violation of expected spatial relationships. This topological illusion demonstrates the limitations of intuitive geometric reasoning and reveals how human spatial cognition relies on implicit assumptions about orientability and boundary conditions. The Möbius strip has become a canonical example in mathematics education and cognitive science for illustrating non-intuitive properties of space. Its perceptual impact derives not from sensory ambiguity but from the conceptual conflict between visual-haptic exploration and learned expectations about surface topology. The phenomenon connects to broader questions about how humans represent and reason about three-dimensional structure, the role of embodied experience in geometric cognition, and the cognitive penetrability of spatial perception.
