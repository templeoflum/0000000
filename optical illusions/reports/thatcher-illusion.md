## Abstract

The Thatcher Illusion, discovered by Peter Thompson in 1980, demonstrates the critical role of configural processing in face perception. When a face is inverted (turned upside down), observers fail to notice grotesque distortions created by locally inverting the eyes and mouth while leaving the face as a whole inverted. However, when the same manipulated face is viewed upright, the distortions become immediately and strikingly apparent, appearing highly abnormal and disturbing. This dramatic difference in detectability reveals that face perception relies heavily on holistic, configural analysis of spatial relationships among facial features, and that this specialized processing is severely disrupted by inversion. The illusion is named after Margaret Thatcher because Thompson's original demonstration used her photograph. The Thatcher Illusion provides compelling evidence for the face inversion effect and supports theories proposing specialized neural mechanisms for upright face processing. The phenomenon demonstrates that while individual facial features can be processed in isolation when faces are inverted, the normal configural integration that allows rapid detection of abnormal spatial relationships is orientation-dependent. Research using the Thatcher Illusion has contributed to understanding the fusiform face area, the distinction between featural and configural processing, developmental trajectories of face expertise, and clinical populations with impaired face processing such as prosopagnosia.
