## Abstract

The Breathing Square Illusion is a motion illusion in which a square shape embedded in a static pattern appears to expand and contract rhythmically, as if breathing or pulsating. Despite the complete absence of physical change in the stimulus, observers perceive periodic variations in the size of the square, with alternating phases of apparent growth and shrinkage creating a dynamic, oscillatory impression. The illusory breathing motion typically occurs at a slow, regular rhythm and can persist for extended viewing periods, though the effect may vary in strength depending on attention, fixation stability, and individual differences. The illusion is often produced by specific arrangements of surrounding context elements, edge configurations, or luminance gradients that interact with the square's boundaries. The phenomenon appears to arise from the visual system's processing of spatial relationships and motion signals, potentially involving adaptation effects, lateral interactions between edge detectors, and the integration of local motion energy signals. Small eye movements may modulate the retinal image in ways that contribute to the perceived pulsation. The breathing square illusion is related to a broader class of dynamic illusions in static patterns and provides evidence for the active, constructive nature of visual perception, where stable perceptual representations emerge from continuous neural processes that can generate spurious motion signals.
