## Abstract

The curvature aftereffect is a shape adaptation phenomenon first systematically described by James J. Gibson in 1933, in which prolonged exposure to curved contours causes subsequently viewed straight lines to appear curved in the opposite direction. After adapting to a convex curve, for instance, a straight line appears concave, and vice versa. This aftereffect demonstrates that the visual system maintains specialized neural mechanisms for encoding curvature as a fundamental property of contour shape, distinct from orientation processing. The magnitude of the aftereffect depends on adaptation duration, curvature magnitude, and the spatial relationship between adaptation and test stimuli. The phenomenon exhibits both retinotopic specificity, indicating involvement of early visual areas, and some degree of transfer across retinal location, suggesting contributions from higher-level shape processing mechanisms. The curvature aftereffect is part of a broader family of figural aftereffects described by Gibson and later researchers, all demonstrating that visual adaptation extends beyond simple features like orientation and color to encompass complex shape properties. Contemporary neurophysiological research has identified curvature-selective neurons in primate visual cortex, providing a neural substrate for this perceptual phenomenon.
