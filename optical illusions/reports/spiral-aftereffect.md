## Abstract

The spiral aftereffect is a motion adaptation phenomenon in which prolonged viewing of a rotating spiral pattern induces a vivid impression of opposite motion in subsequently viewed stationary patterns, causing them to appear to expand, contract, or rotate. When a spiral rotates to create an impression of inward motion (contraction), stationary test patterns subsequently appear to expand outward; conversely, adaptation to outward spiral motion produces aftereffects of contraction. The phenomenon is closely related to Plateau's spiral and represents a specific instance of the broader class of motion aftereffects. The spiral aftereffect is particularly compelling because the induced motion is complex and radial rather than simple linear translation, and because it can produce striking illusory motion in natural objects and scenes. The effect demonstrates that the visual system contains neural mechanisms selective for complex optic flow patterns including radial expansion and contraction, which are ecologically important for navigation and depth perception. The magnitude and duration of the aftereffect scale with adaptation time and spiral rotation speed. The spiral aftereffect has been extensively studied in psychophysical and neurophysiological research on motion processing, revealing that motion adaptation occurs in multiple cortical areas including V1, MT, and MST, with different areas contributing to different aspects of complex motion perception.
