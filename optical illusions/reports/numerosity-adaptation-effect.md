## Abstract

The numerosity adaptation effect demonstrates that the visual system's representation of number is subject to adaptation, analogous to adaptation for low-level features like orientation and motion. After prolonged exposure to displays containing a large number of elements (e.g., many dots), a subsequently viewed display with a moderate number of elements is perceived as containing fewer items than it actually does. Conversely, adaptation to sparse displays causes moderate-numerosity displays to appear more numerous. The effect operates over the subitizing range (1-4 items) and extends into the estimation range (5-10+ items), suggesting that adaptation affects multiple numerosity processing mechanisms. The magnitude of the aftereffect depends on the ratio between adaptation and test numerosities, consistent with Weber's law for number perception. Critically, the numerosity adaptation effect persists when controlling for non-numerical confounds such as total area, density, and convex hull, indicating that it reflects adaptation of genuine numerosity-selective neural mechanisms rather than adaptation to correlated visual features. The phenomenon provides strong evidence that numerosity is encoded as a primary visual property by specialized neural populations, likely located in parietal cortex. Numerosity adaptation has become an important tool for investigating the neural basis of numerical cognition and its relationship to symbolic mathematical abilities.
