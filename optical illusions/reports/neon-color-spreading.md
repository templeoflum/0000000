## Abstract

Neon color spreading is a striking chromatic completion phenomenon first systematically described by Dario Varin in 1971, in which illusory color appears to spread from colored line segments into adjacent achromatic regions, creating a translucent, neon-like wash of color. The classic configuration consists of a grid of black lines interrupted by colored segments at their intersections; the colored segments appear to induce a diffuse colored surface that extends across the enclosed spaces while maintaining perceptual transparency. The effect is strongest when the colored segments are collinear with implied contours and when the background and enclosed regions share similar luminance. Unlike simple simultaneous contrast, neon spreading produces a qualitatively distinct percept of a semi-transparent colored surface floating above or in front of the background. The magnitude of spreading depends on spatial parameters including line width, gap size, and geometric configuration. Neon color spreading reveals fundamental mechanisms of perceptual surface completion and figure-ground organization, demonstrating that the visual system actively constructs surface representations from fragmentary contour information. Neural models propose that the effect arises from filling-in processes in early visual cortex, where chromatic signals propagate within regions bounded by contours, combined with transparency computations in mid-level vision.
