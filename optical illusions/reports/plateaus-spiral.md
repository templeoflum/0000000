## Abstract

Plateau's spiral, discovered by Belgian physicist Joseph Plateau in 1850, is a motion aftereffect phenomenon in which adaptation to a rotating spiral induces a compelling illusion of expansion or contraction in subsequently viewed stationary objects. When a spiral pattern rotates clockwise (appearing to contract toward the center), adaptation causes stationary patterns to appear to expand outward; counterclockwise rotation (apparent expansion) produces aftereffects of contraction. The phenomenon is a specific instance of the motion aftereffect, but unlike simple translational motion aftereffects, the spiral aftereffect involves radial or rotational motion components. The illusion demonstrates that the visual system contains motion detectors selective for complex motion patterns including expansion, contraction, and rotation, not just simple directional translation. The magnitude and duration of the aftereffect depend on adaptation time, spiral rotation speed, and the spatial frequency content of test stimuli. Plateau's spiral was one of the earliest systematic demonstrations of motion adaptation and played a significant historical role in establishing that motion perception involves neural mechanisms subject to fatigue and adaptation. The phenomenon remains a popular demonstration in vision science education and has been used extensively in psychophysical investigations of motion processing mechanisms.
