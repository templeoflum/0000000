## Abstract

The Reversing Depth Illusion encompasses a class of visual phenomena in which the perceived depth organization of a stimulus spontaneously alternates between two mutually exclusive three-dimensional interpretations, despite unchanging retinal input. Classic examples include hollow face masks that flip between appearing concave and convex, architectural drawings such as the Necker cube that reverse between different depth configurations, and ambiguous wire-frame figures that alternate between competing spatial arrangements. These reversals occur because the two-dimensional retinal projection is compatible with multiple three-dimensional structures, and the visual system lacks sufficient information to uniquely determine the correct depth interpretation. The perceptual system typically settles on one interpretation at a time, but this solution is unstable, leading to periodic spontaneous reversals. The phenomenon demonstrates that depth perception involves active inference processes that generate hypotheses about three-dimensional structure from ambiguous two-dimensional input. Factors influencing reversal rates include attention, prior knowledge, viewing duration, and stimulus characteristics such as contrast and spatial frequency. Reversing depth illusions reveal the constructive nature of visual perception and provide experimental paradigms for investigating the neural mechanisms of perceptual bistability, including the role of top-down processes, predictive coding, and competitive dynamics in cortical networks responsible for depth processing.
