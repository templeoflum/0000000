## Abstract

The T illusion is a simple yet robust geometric illusion in which a vertical line segment bisecting a horizontal line segment of equal length appears longer than the horizontal component. The stimulus configuration resembles an inverted T or plus sign, with the vertical element systematically overestimated in length relative to the horizontal element by approximately 10-20 percent in typical viewing conditions. The T illusion is considered a specific manifestation of the more general vertical-horizontal illusion, in which vertical extents are perceived as longer than equal horizontal extents. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain this anisotropy: differential neural representation of vertical versus horizontal orientations in early visual cortex, with vertical orientations potentially commanding greater cortical resources or attention; scanning patterns or eye movement biases that favor vertical exploration; and ecological factors related to depth interpretation, where vertical extents in natural scenes are more variable and informationally rich. The illusion persists across diverse stimulus variations, including variations in line thickness, color, and surrounding context, suggesting involvement of early, automatic visual processing stages. The T illusion has been studied in developmental populations, cross-cultural contexts, and clinical groups, providing insights into how orientation anisotropies emerge and are modulated by experience and neural architecture.
