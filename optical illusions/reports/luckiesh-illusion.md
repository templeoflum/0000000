## Abstract

The Luckiesh Illusion refers to geometric visual distortions documented by Matthew Luckiesh, an American physicist and illumination engineer who made significant contributions to visual perception research in the early-to-mid 20th century. Luckiesh's work at the General Electric laboratories focused on the relationship between lighting conditions and visual perception, and he catalogued numerous geometric illusions in his comprehensive texts on visual phenomena. The illusion typically involves configurations where straight lines or geometric shapes appear distorted when placed against specific background patterns or in particular spatial arrangements. The perceptual effect demonstrates how contextual elements influence the perceived geometry of target stimuli, consistent with principles of lateral interaction and spatial filtering in early visual processing. Theoretical accounts emphasize the role of orientation-selective neurons in V1 and their susceptibility to contextual modulation from surrounding contours. The illusion exemplifies broader principles of how the visual system integrates local features with global context, sometimes producing systematic misperceptions of geometric properties. Luckiesh's systematic documentation of such phenomena contributed to the foundation of modern visual psychophysics and remains relevant to understanding the computational constraints of spatial vision.
