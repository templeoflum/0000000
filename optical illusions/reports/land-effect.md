## Abstract

The Land effect, discovered by Polaroid founder Edwin Land in 1959, demonstrates that observers perceive a surprisingly rich and differentiated range of colors when viewing images projected through only two wavelength bands rather than the full visible spectrum. In Land's original demonstrations, he superimposed two black-and-white photographs of the same scene taken through different color filters, then projected them using long-wavelength (red) and middle-wavelength (green or white) light. Despite the impoverished spectral information available, observers reported seeing blues, yellows, greens, and other hues that were not physically present in the projection. This phenomenon revealed that color perception depends critically on spatial relationships and relative reflectances across a scene rather than absolute wavelength composition at each point. The effect provided foundational evidence for Land's Retinex theory of color vision, which proposes that the visual system computes surface color by comparing reflectances across spatial regions within separate chromatic channels. The Land effect has profoundly influenced computational models of color constancy and demonstrated that human color vision operates through sophisticated contextual processing rather than simple wavelength detection. The illusion remains a cornerstone demonstration of the constructive and relational nature of chromatic perception.
