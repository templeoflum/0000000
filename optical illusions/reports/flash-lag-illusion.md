## Abstract

The Flash Lag Illusion is a temporal mislocalization phenomenon in which a briefly flashed stationary stimulus appears to spatially lag behind a continuously moving object, even when the two are physically aligned at the moment of the flash. When observers view a moving object and a brief flash is presented at the same spatial location as the moving object, they consistently report that the flash appears behind the moving object in the direction opposite to motion. This perceptual offset typically ranges from several degrees of visual angle and occurs reliably across diverse stimulus configurations and motion parameters. The illusion reveals fundamental aspects of how the visual system processes and integrates temporal information from moving versus stationary stimuli. Several competing theoretical accounts have been proposed to explain the phenomenon, including differential latency hypotheses suggesting faster processing of moving stimuli, predictive mechanisms that extrapolate moving object positions forward in time, and postdictive accounts involving temporal integration windows. The Flash Lag Illusion has become a central paradigm for investigating the neural timing of visual perception, the relationship between motion processing and position encoding, and whether perception reflects past sensory input or incorporates predictions about future states.
