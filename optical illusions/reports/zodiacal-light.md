## Abstract

Zodiacal light is a faint, diffuse triangular or cone-shaped glow extending along the ecliptic plane in the night sky, most visible near the horizon after evening twilight or before morning twilight, caused by sunlight scattered from interplanetary dust particles concentrated in the plane of the solar system. This phenomenon results from forward scattering of sunlight by micron-sized dust grains distributed throughout the inner solar system, with the dust concentration and thus brightness decreasing with distance from the sun. The zodiacal light appears brightest near the horizon along the ecliptic, tapering to an apex extending 60 to 90 degrees or more from the sun's position below the horizon. Its name derives from its appearance along the zodiacal constellations that lie near the ecliptic plane. The phenomenon is most readily observable from tropical and subtropical latitudes where the ecliptic makes a steep angle with the horizon during appropriate seasons, and requires dark, clear skies far from light pollution. A related phenomenon, the gegenschein or counterglow, appears as a faint brightening along the ecliptic directly opposite the sun, caused by backscattering from interplanetary dust at the antisolar point. Historical observations of zodiacal light contributed to understanding the solar system's structure and the distribution of interplanetary matter, while modern studies inform models of dust dynamics, composition, and sources including asteroid collisions and cometary debris.
