## Abstract

The Penrose Stairs, also known as the Impossible Staircase, is an impossible object created by geneticist Lionel Penrose and his mathematician son Roger Penrose in 1959. The figure depicts a staircase that forms a closed loop while maintaining a consistent upward or downward incline throughout its circuit, creating a paradoxical structure where one could ascend or descend indefinitely without ever changing elevation. The illusion exploits depth cue conflicts in two-dimensional representations of three-dimensional space, where local depth relationships appear geometrically consistent but global integration reveals violations of Euclidean geometry. Each individual step appears properly connected to its neighbors with appropriate rise and tread dimensions, and the perspective cues suggest a normal architectural staircase. However, following the complete circuit around the four-sided structure reveals that the stairs return to the starting point despite maintaining constant elevation change in a single direction. This creates an impossible closed loop analogous to a perpetual motion machine in the domain of spatial relationships. The Penrose Stairs gained widespread recognition after M.C. Escher incorporated the concept into his 1960 lithograph Ascending and Descending, where robed figures perpetually climb or descend the impossible staircase. The figure demonstrates fundamental principles about how the visual system uses local depth cues to construct global three-dimensional interpretations and how these local-to-global integration processes can be exploited to create compelling spatial paradoxes. The Penrose Stairs has become one of the most iconic impossible objects in both art and perceptual science.
