## Abstract

The color afterimage is one of the most fundamental and readily demonstrable visual aftereffects, occurring when prolonged viewing of a colored stimulus produces a subsequent perception of its complementary color when viewing a neutral surface. This phenomenon manifests in two primary forms: negative afterimages, where the perceived color is complementary to the adapting stimulus, and positive afterimages, where the perceived color matches the original stimulus. Negative afterimages arise from photoreceptor and neural adaptation in the retina and early visual cortex, where prolonged exposure to specific wavelengths of light causes selective fatigue of the corresponding cone photoreceptors and chromatic opponent channels. The effect is strongest immediately after adaptation and decays exponentially over several seconds. Color afterimages demonstrate fundamental principles of color vision including opponent-process theory and the adaptive nature of sensory systems. They can be influenced by factors such as adaptation duration, stimulus intensity, background luminance, and eye movements. The phenomenon has been known since antiquity and played a crucial role in the development of color vision theories in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
