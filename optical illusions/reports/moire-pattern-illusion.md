## Abstract

Moiré Pattern Illusions arise when two or more repetitive patterns, such as grids, lines, or dots, are superimposed with a slight offset in angle, spacing, or phase. The interference between these overlapping patterns creates emergent large-scale structures that were not present in either original pattern alone. These emergent structures can include apparent bands, waves, or curved contours that may appear to shimmer, flow, or move when the observer or the patterns shift position. When viewed dynamically, such as when one pattern slides relative to another, moiré effects can produce compelling illusory motion that greatly exceeds the actual physical displacement. The spatial frequency of the resulting moiré pattern is typically much lower than that of the constituent patterns, making the emergent structures highly visible and often striking. Moiré phenomena occur in diverse contexts including printed materials, digital displays, architecture, and artistic compositions. The perceptual effects arise from the spatial filtering and interference characteristics of overlapping repetitive structures, which create beat frequencies analogous to acoustic beat phenomena. Motion-sensitive mechanisms in the visual system respond strongly to the slow-frequency moiré patterns, generating motion percepts when these patterns shift. Moiré Pattern Illusions demonstrate fundamental principles of spatial frequency analysis and have practical implications for display design, printing technology, and artistic applications including op art.
