## Abstract

The Leaning Tower Illusion is a compelling geometric illusion discovered by Frederick Kingdom and colleagues in 2007, in which two identical side-by-side photographs of a receding object—such as the Leaning Tower of Pisa—appear to diverge, making one tower seem to lean more dramatically than the other. The illusion occurs because the visual system expects that two parallel structures receding into the distance should converge in the retinal image due to linear perspective. When presented with two identical images showing parallel vertical edges, the brain interprets them as representing two different viewing angles or physical configurations rather than identical copies. The misapplication of size and shape constancy mechanisms causes the towers to appear to diverge rather than remain parallel. This illusion demonstrates the automatic and mandatory nature of perspective processing in human vision: even though observers know intellectually that the two images are identical, the perceptual system applies perspective corrections based on implicit assumptions about three-dimensional scene structure. The Leaning Tower Illusion has significance for understanding how the visual system integrates multiple cues to depth and applies constancy operations, and it highlights the challenges faced when reconciling conflicting depth information from identical pictorial representations placed side by side.
