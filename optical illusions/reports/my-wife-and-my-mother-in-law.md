## Abstract

The "My Wife and My Mother-in-Law" illusion, also known as "Boring's Illusion" or the "Young Girl/Old Woman" figure, is one of the most famous ambiguous images in perceptual psychology. Originally published by William Ely Hill in 1915, the drawing can be perceived as either a young woman looking away or an elderly woman's profile, with identical contours serving different anatomical features in each interpretation. The young woman's chin becomes the old woman's nose, the young woman's ear becomes the old woman's eye, and the choker necklace transforms into a mouth. This bistable figure demonstrates the profound role of perceptual organization in constructing coherent interpretations from ambiguous sensory input. Edwin Boring's extensive research on this illusion in the 1930s established it as a paradigmatic example of how expectations, prior experience, and attentional focus influence perceptual outcomes. The spontaneous alternations between interpretations reveal neural competition processes that prevent stable maintenance of a single percept. Research using this illusion has advanced understanding of top-down influences in perception, the dynamics of perceptual rivalry, and how semantic context can bias initial interpretation. The figure continues to be widely used in cognitive psychology and neuroscience research investigating consciousness, attention, and the neural basis of ambiguous figure perception.
