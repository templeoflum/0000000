## Abstract

The Corridor Illusion is a depth-based size illusion in which identical objects positioned at different locations along a depicted corridor with linear perspective cues appear to differ substantially in size. Typically, an object placed at the apparent far end of the corridor, where perspective convergence suggests greater distance, appears larger than an identical object at the near end, despite both having the same retinal size. This compelling effect demonstrates the automatic operation of size constancy scaling mechanisms that normally compensate for the reduction in retinal image size with increasing distance. In the Corridor Illusion, these mechanisms are misapplied because the depth cues from the perspective rendering conflict with the actual flat presentation of the stimulus. The visual system interprets the object at the apparent far end as being more distant and therefore scales up its perceived size accordingly, while the near object receives less constancy scaling. The illusion provides strong evidence for the mandatory nature of size constancy computations and reveals how pictorial depth cues are sufficient to engage these mechanisms even when binocular and motion parallax cues indicate a flat surface. The magnitude of the effect varies with the strength of perspective cues, the position of objects within the corridor, and individual differences in susceptibility to pictorial depth information.
