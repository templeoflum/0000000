## Abstract

The Ehrenstein Illusion, discovered by German psychologist Walter Ehrenstein in 1941, is a multifaceted phenomenon combining illusory contour formation, brightness enhancement, and size distortion. The classic configuration consists of radial black lines arranged like spokes around a central region, with the lines terminating before reaching a common center point. Observers perceive an illusory bright circle or disk at the center where no physical luminance difference exists, demonstrating spontaneous surface completion by the visual system. Additionally, the physical gaps between the line endpoints and the center appear larger than they actually are, revealing a systematic size distortion component. The brightness enhancement at the center has been attributed to lateral inhibition mechanisms, whereby the radial lines induce reduced neural response in the surrounding region, making the central area appear relatively brighter through disinhibition. The illusory contour aspect reflects surface completion processes that interpolate boundaries based on line terminations. The size distortion may result from interactions between position coding and the neural representations of the radial lines. The Ehrenstein Illusion thus provides a rich testbed for investigating interactions between brightness perception, contour completion, and geometric distortion, with implications for understanding how the visual system constructs unified percepts from local edge information.
