## Abstract

Rubin's Vase, introduced by Danish psychologist Edgar Rubin in 1915, is the paradigmatic demonstration of figure-ground organization and perceptual bistability. The image consists of a black-and-white design that can be perceived either as a white vase against a black background or as two black face profiles facing each other against a white background. Critically, observers cannot perceive both interpretations simultaneously—the visual system must designate one region as "figure" and the other as "ground," with the shared contour belonging exclusively to the figural region. When the percept switches, the contour ownership reverses, and what was previously seen as shapeless background becomes a shaped figure with the contour defining its boundary. This fundamental ambiguity demonstrates that figure-ground segregation is an active organizational process rather than a passive response to stimulus features. Rubin's systematic studies established key principles of figure-ground organization, including that enclosed regions, symmetrical regions, and smaller regions tend to be perceived as figure. The vase illusion has become foundational for research on perceptual organization, attentional selection, and the neural mechanisms underlying figure-ground segregation. Modern neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies have used this stimulus to identify cortical areas sensitive to figure-ground assignment and to trace the time course of organizational processing in the visual system.
