## Abstract

Weber's Illusion describes a tactile distance perception phenomenon in which a moving stimulus traced across the skin is perceived as covering significantly more distance than an objectively equal static spatial extent presented simultaneously or sequentially. Discovered by Ernst Heinrich Weber, a pioneering figure in experimental psychology and psychophysics, this illusion reveals fundamental properties of somatosensory spatial processing and the integration of tactile and kinesthetic information. The effect demonstrates that perceived spatial extent in touch is not simply determined by the spatial separation of stimulated receptors, but is enhanced by the temporal dynamics and motion trajectory of stimulation. Weber's Illusion connects to broader principles of sensory magnitude estimation, showing that movement amplifies perceived spatial extent in the tactile domain, possibly due to the recruitment of additional neural populations responsive to motion, increased attentional engagement, or integration of proprioceptive signals about limb position changes during tracking. The illusion has implications for understanding haptic exploration, tactile spatial acuity, and the relationship between static and dynamic touch. Research on Weber's Illusion intersects with studies of tactile motion perception, receptor field properties in somatosensory cortex, and cross-modal interactions between touch, vision, and proprioception in constructing coherent spatial representations of the body and near-body space.
