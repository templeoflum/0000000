## Abstract

The Judd Illusion is a variant of the classic Müller-Lyer illusion, discovered by American psychologist Charles Hubbard Judd in 1899. While the standard Müller-Lyer illusion uses arrowheads or fins pointing in opposite directions at the two ends of a line, the Judd variant features arrowheads pointing in the same direction at both ends. This configuration creates a compelling illusion where the physical midpoint of the line appears displaced or shifted from its actual position. The illusion demonstrates that the perception of position and extent is systematically influenced by the orientation and direction of contextual elements, not merely by their presence or absence. Judd's work on this illusion contributed to early debates about the mechanisms underlying geometric illusions, particularly whether they arise from eye movement patterns, depth cue misinterpretation, or low-level neural processing. The consistent directional bias in perceived midpoint location suggests that the visual system integrates information about line orientation and terminator features in ways that systematically distort spatial representations. The Judd Illusion has been used to test theories of size and position perception and provides evidence for the role of contextual features in determining both extent and location judgments. It remains relevant to contemporary models of spatial coding and attentional distribution in visual perception.
