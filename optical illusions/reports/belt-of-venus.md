## Abstract

The Belt of Venus, also known as the Anti-Twilight Arch, is a distinctive atmospheric optical phenomenon visible as a pinkish or reddish band appearing above the horizon opposite the sun during twilight periods. This colored band typically extends 10 to 20 degrees above the horizon and is bounded below by the dark bluish-grey Earth's shadow and above by the ordinary blue sky. The characteristic pink or rose coloration arises from backscattering of reddened sunlight by atmospheric particles: during twilight, when the sun is below the horizon, its light must travel through a greater thickness of atmosphere, preferentially scattering blue wavelengths and allowing red wavelengths to propagate. These red wavelengths are then backscattered by atmospheric dust and aerosols in the region opposite the sun, creating the distinctive pinkish glow. The phenomenon is named after Venus, the Roman goddess of love, due to its romantic rose-colored appearance. The Belt of Venus is most clearly visible during clear atmospheric conditions approximately 15 to 30 minutes before sunrise or after sunset, when the sun is positioned 4 to 6 degrees below the horizon, providing optimal geometric conditions for the backscattering effect.
