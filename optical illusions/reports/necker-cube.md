## Abstract

The Necker Cube, discovered by Swiss crystallographer Louis Albert Necker in 1832, is a wireframe drawing of a cube that spontaneously reverses its apparent depth orientation during sustained viewing. This classic ambiguous figure can be perceived with either the lower-left square or the upper-right square appearing as the front face, with the entire three-dimensional interpretation flipping between these two configurations every few seconds. The illusion occurs because the two-dimensional line drawing lacks definitive depth cues that would specify which interpretation is correct, allowing the visual system to construct two equally valid three-dimensional percepts from the same retinal input. Necker's original observation emerged while examining rhombohedral crystals in drawings, where he noticed the spontaneous reversals of apparent spatial orientation. The phenomenon demonstrates fundamental principles of perceptual organization, particularly how the visual system actively imposes structure on ambiguous sensory data. The Necker Cube has become one of the most extensively studied bistable stimuli in vision science, providing a paradigmatic case for investigating neural mechanisms of perceptual rivalry, the role of attention in stabilizing percepts, and the dynamics of conscious awareness. Research using this illusion has contributed substantially to understanding how competing neural representations interact and how voluntary control can influence, but not completely suppress, spontaneous perceptual alternations.
