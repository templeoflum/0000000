## Abstract

The Helmholtz Illusion, also known as the Irradiation Illusion, encompasses two related perceptual phenomena described by German physicist and physiologist Hermann von Helmholtz in 1867. The first variant demonstrates that bright or white regions appear larger than physically equal dark regions, a phenomenon Helmholtz termed "irradiation." This effect occurs because light areas seem to encroach upon adjacent dark areas, causing the bright region to appear expanded. The second variant involves filled geometric shapes: a square filled with horizontal lines appears taller and narrower than an identical square filled with vertical lines, which appears wider and shorter. Both phenomena reflect fundamental properties of early visual processing, particularly lateral inhibition and spatial filtering mechanisms in the retina and primary visual cortex. Light spreads in the optical system and neural responses, causing bright stimuli to activate a broader area of the retina and cortex than their physical extent would predict. The orientation-dependent variant arises from anisotropic spatial filtering, where the visual system processes horizontal and vertical information differently. The Helmholtz Illusion has historical importance in establishing the physiological basis of perception and continues to inform contemporary models of lightness perception, contour processing, and size estimation.
