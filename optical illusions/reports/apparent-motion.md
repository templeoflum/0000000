## Abstract

Apparent Motion, also known as Beta Movement, is a fundamental perceptual phenomenon in which the sequential presentation of static stimuli at different spatial locations produces the compelling impression of smooth, continuous motion. First systematically investigated by Max Wertheimer in 1912, this illusion forms one of the cornerstone observations of Gestalt psychology and has profound implications for understanding visual motion perception. When two stationary images are flashed in rapid succession at nearby locations, observers do not perceive two separate events but rather a single object moving seamlessly from the first position to the second. The strength and quality of the perceived motion depend critically on the temporal interval between presentations (interstimulus interval) and the spatial separation between the stimuli. Optimal apparent motion occurs within specific spatiotemporal windows, typically with interstimulus intervals between 30 and 200 milliseconds. This phenomenon underlies motion picture technology, animation, and electronic displays, where discrete static frames create the experience of fluid movement. The study of apparent motion has been instrumental in revealing the constructive nature of visual perception and the temporal integration processes that bridge discrete sensory inputs into coherent perceptual experiences.
