## Abstract

The Kanizsa triangle, introduced by Italian psychologist Gaetano Kanizsa in 1955, is among the most iconic and extensively studied examples of illusory contour formation and modal surface completion. The classic configuration consists of three black circular discs with wedge-shaped notches removed, positioned at the vertices of an imaginary triangle, often accompanied by three angle brackets with their vertices pointing inward. Despite the absence of any physical luminance gradient defining the triangle's edges, observers perceive a vivid white equilateral triangle that appears to float above the inducing elements, with clearly defined illusory contours along its three sides and a subjective impression that the illusory triangle is slightly brighter than the background. This perceived brightness difference, though reported consistently by observers, is not physically present in the stimulus. The Kanizsa triangle demonstrates both contour interpolation and surface filling-in, revealing how the visual system constructs explicit representations of edges and surfaces based on the geometric relationships among spatially separated inducing features. The illusion has been investigated extensively using psychophysical methods, neurophysiological recordings, and computational modeling, providing crucial insights into the mechanisms underlying perceptual completion, figure-ground organization, and the neural basis of subjective visual experience.
