## Abstract

The Silhouette Illusion, also known as the "Spinning Dancer," is a bistable apparent motion display created by Japanese web designer Nobuyuki Kayahara in 2003. The animation shows the silhouette of a pirouetting dancer that can be perceived as rotating either clockwise or counterclockwise, with spontaneous reversals occurring during sustained viewing. The ambiguity arises because the silhouette lacks depth cues such as shading, texture, and binocular disparity—all information that would normally specify whether a limb is in front of or behind the body. Without these depth signals, the two-dimensional projection of the rotating figure is compatible with two opposite rotation directions, leading to bistable perception. When observers perceive a switch in rotation direction, the entire three-dimensional interpretation of the dancer's orientation and limb positions reorganizes simultaneously. The illusion demonstrates how motion perception depends on inferred three-dimensional structure, not just retinal motion signals. Despite popular claims that the perceived rotation direction reveals brain dominance or cognitive style, scientific evidence does not support these interpretations. Research using this illusion has contributed to understanding structure-from-motion perception, the role of depth ambiguity in motion bistability, and the neural mechanisms underlying perceptual alternations in dynamic displays. The Spinning Dancer remains one of the most widely disseminated examples of bistable perception in popular culture.
