## Abstract

The Moon Illusion is one of the oldest documented perceptual phenomena, in which the moon appears substantially larger when positioned near the horizon compared to when it is elevated high in the sky, despite subtending essentially the same visual angle (approximately 0.5 degrees) in both positions. This dramatic size difference has been noted since antiquity, with references appearing in ancient Greek, Chinese, and Arabic texts. The illusion persists even when observers are aware of the objective equality of the moon's angular size, demonstrating its resistance to cognitive override. Multiple theoretical accounts have been proposed over centuries of investigation, with the most prominent involving misapplied size constancy scaling driven by depth cues. The apparent distance hypothesis suggests that the horizon moon appears more distant due to intervening terrain cues, and size constancy mechanisms inappropriately scale its perceived size upward. Alternative accounts invoke the shape of perceived visual space, relative size comparisons with terrestrial objects, and oculomotor factors. Despite extensive research, the Moon Illusion remains incompletely explained, with evidence supporting multiple contributing factors. The phenomenon is significant for understanding how the visual system integrates retinal information with contextual depth cues and highlights the ecological constraints that shaped size perception mechanisms.
