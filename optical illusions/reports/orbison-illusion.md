## Abstract

The Orbison Illusion, first reported by William Dillard Orbison in 1939, occurs when regular geometric shapes such as squares, circles, or rectangles are superimposed on backgrounds containing radiating lines or concentric patterns. Under these conditions, the target shapes appear systematically distorted, with straight edges appearing curved and right angles appearing acute or obtuse. The magnitude of distortion depends on the relationship between the target shape's orientation and the background pattern's radial structure. Circles appear elliptical, squares appear trapezoidal, and the overall effect creates a compelling impression of spatial warping around the background's focal point. The illusion exemplifies a broader class of context-dependent geometric distortions where background patterns influence the perceived shape of foreground elements. Theoretical accounts emphasize the role of orientation-selective neurons in primary visual cortex and their susceptibility to contextual modulation from surrounding contours. The radiating background may also evoke depth processing mechanisms, with the pattern resembling perspective cues, leading to misapplied size constancy scaling. Computational models have demonstrated that spatial filtering operations sensitive to local orientation gradients can produce the observed distortions. The Orbison Illusion remains important for understanding figure-ground interactions and the limitations of shape constancy mechanisms, revealing how context profoundly influences geometric perception even for simple, familiar forms.
