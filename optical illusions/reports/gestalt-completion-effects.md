## Abstract

Gestalt completion effects encompass a broad class of perceptual phenomena in which the visual system spontaneously organizes fragmentary or incomplete visual elements into coherent wholes based on organizational principles identified by Gestalt psychologists in the early twentieth century. These principles include proximity, similarity, good continuation, closure, common fate, and prägnanz (the tendency toward simple, regular forms). When presented with scattered dots, broken lines, or partially occluded shapes, observers automatically perceive organized patterns, continuous contours, and complete objects rather than experiencing the stimulus elements as independent, unrelated fragments. Gestalt completion operates across multiple levels of visual processing, from basic contour integration to high-level object recognition, and reflects fundamental constraints on how the visual system resolves ambiguity and constructs stable perceptual representations from noisy sensory input. These effects demonstrate that perception is not a passive registration of sensory data but an active process of hypothesis formation and constraint satisfaction. Modern neuroscience and computational vision have provided mechanistic accounts of many Gestalt phenomena in terms of neural circuits implementing probabilistic inference, lateral interactions among feature detectors, and feedback from higher cortical areas. Gestalt completion effects remain central to understanding perceptual organization and continue to inform research on attention, grouping, and scene segmentation.
