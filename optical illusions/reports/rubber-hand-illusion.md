## Abstract

The rubber hand illusion is a powerful demonstration of multisensory integration in bodily self-consciousness, first systematically described by Botvinick and Cohen in 1998. The illusion is induced by simultaneously stroking a visible rubber hand and the participant's own hidden hand with synchronous tactile stimulation. After a period typically lasting one to two minutes, participants report experiencing the tactile sensations as arising from the rubber hand and often feel a sense of ownership over the artificial appendage. This illusory ownership can be objectively measured through proprioceptive drift, where participants mislocalize their hidden hand toward the position of the rubber hand, and through physiological responses such as skin conductance changes when the rubber hand is threatened. The effect requires temporal synchrony between visual and tactile stimulation, anatomical plausibility in the rubber hand's position and orientation, and structural correspondence between the artificial and real hand. The rubber hand illusion reveals that body ownership is not fixed but dynamically constructed through the integration of multisensory signals according to Bayesian principles that weight the relative reliability of different cues. Individual differences in susceptibility correlate with factors including interoceptive awareness, schizotypy, and empathy, making the paradigm valuable for investigating both normal and disrupted body representation.
