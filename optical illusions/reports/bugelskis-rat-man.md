## Abstract

Bugelski's Rat-Man is an ambiguous figure that can be perceived as either a rat or a man's face wearing glasses, depending on the observer's perceptual set and prior context. Created by B.R. Bugelski and Daphne Alampay in 1961, this stimulus became a classic demonstration of how top-down expectations and priming influence perceptual interpretation of ambiguous visual information. In their seminal experiments, participants who were first shown a series of animal pictures were significantly more likely to perceive the ambiguous figure as a rat, while those primed with human faces were more likely to see a man. The illusion consists of carefully arranged black regions that can be organized into either interpretation: the rat's body and head can alternatively be seen as a man's hair and face, with the rat's ears becoming eyeglasses. This reversible figure demonstrates that perception is not simply a bottom-up process of feature detection but involves substantial top-down influence from expectations, prior knowledge, and contextual information. The Rat-Man illusion has been widely used in cognitive psychology to study perceptual set, priming effects, and the interaction between sensory input and cognitive expectations in constructing conscious visual experience.
