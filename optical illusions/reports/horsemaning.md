## Abstract

Horsemaning is a photographic technique and internet meme in which two people position themselves to create the visual illusion of a single decapitated body, with one person showing only their headless torso while a second person positions their head to appear separated from the body. The name references the Headless Horseman of folklore and became popular as a revival of a 1920s photography fad. This phenomenon exploits several principles of visual perception, including the visual system's tendency to group spatially proximate elements into unified objects, the limited depth information available in two-dimensional photographs, and top-down expectations about human body configuration that can be violated through careful staging. The illusion depends on precise alignment between the hidden body of the person providing the head and the visible torso of the person whose head is concealed, typically achieved through positioning relative to occlusion boundaries such as walls, furniture, or careful framing. Horsemaning demonstrates how photographic images remove critical depth cues available in natural viewing, including binocular disparity and motion parallax, making the visual system more susceptible to spatial misinterpretations. The phenomenon illustrates the broader category of perspective-based photographic illusions that exploit the two-dimensional projection inherent in photography to create impossible or improbable spatial configurations that would be immediately apparent in three-dimensional viewing.
