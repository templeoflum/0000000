## Abstract

The Glare Effect, also known as the Luminosity Illusion, occurs when a bright region surrounded by darkness appears to glow or emit light rather than simply reflecting it. This phenomenon represents a failure or extension of the visual system's ability to distinguish between luminous (self-emitting) and illuminated (light-reflecting) surfaces. In typical viewing conditions, the visual system is remarkably adept at making this distinction, a capacity critical for understanding material properties and light source locations in the environment. However, under specific stimulus configurations, particularly when a bright region has soft or blurred edges and is embedded in a dark surround, observers perceive the region as luminous rather than as a brightly illuminated surface. This illusion is related to the perception of neon color spreading and certain classes of transparency phenomena. The effect may involve spatial filtering processes that respond to the gradient profile at the boundary between the bright region and its surround, with shallow gradients promoting luminosity percepts. From a Bayesian perspective, the visual system may be inferring the most probable interpretation of the retinal image, and under conditions where a self-luminous source is a more likely explanation than an improbably bright reflection, the luminosity percept emerges. The Glare Effect demonstrates the sophisticated inferences the visual system makes about material properties and illumination, revealing the boundary conditions under which these inferences can be misleading.
