## Abstract

Hybrid images are visual stimuli that combine high and low spatial frequency information from two different source images to create a single composite image whose perceived content changes systematically with viewing distance. The technique involves extracting high spatial frequencies from one image and low spatial frequencies from another, then superimposing them. When viewed up close, the high-frequency details dominate perception and observers see one image. When viewed from several feet away, the high frequencies become less visible due to the limited spatial resolution of peripheral vision and distance-dependent contrast sensitivity, causing the low-frequency structure to dominate and revealing a completely different image. Developed by Aude Oliva and Philippe Schyns in 2006, hybrid images demonstrate that visual perception operates across multiple spatial scales simultaneously and that different frequency bands can carry independent semantic content. The effect reveals the hierarchical nature of visual processing and shows that scene recognition can proceed from coarse-to-fine or fine-to-coarse depending on viewing conditions. Hybrid images have become important tools for studying spatial frequency processing, attention, and the neural bases of object and scene recognition.
